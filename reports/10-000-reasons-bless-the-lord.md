## Thesis

The singer tells his own soul to bless the Lord and worship His holy name, every morning, all through life, and on past death forever. God is rich in love, slow to anger, great in name, and kind, and His goodness gives the singer ten thousand reasons to keep singing.

---

## Overall recommendation

Weak

Do not feature it. The church has logged it 6 times across at least four years of the 2023–2026 window, and the room loves it. The chorus opens with Psalm 103:1, and the second verse borrows Psalm 103:8. But the song names ten thousand reasons to bless the Lord and gives the congregation almost none of them. Psalm 103 itself lists forgiveness, redemption from the pit, and sins removed as far as the east is from the west. The song leaves all of that out. Christ, sin, and the cross are never named, and most of the lyric is about the singer's own singing. It thrives because it is easy, warm, and broadly true, which is the calibration line for Weak.

---

## Alternative song suggestions

The lyric is reaching for Psalm 103: a soul stirred to bless the Lord for His mercy, from morning to the day of death. Three of these the church already sings. One is not in the catalog.

**Already sung**

- **Bless the Lord, O My Soul (Psalm 103)** (Zach Jones, Sovereign Grace, CCLI 4764203, graded Strong). Logged 7 times. It is the same psalm, with the psalm's own reasons kept in: sins forgiven and removed, the pit redeemed, the Lord's compassion like a father's.
- **Great Is Thy Faithfulness** (Thomas O. Chisholm / William M. Runyan, hymn, CCLI 18723, logged 5 times; grade pending). It covers the first verse's theme of new mercies each morning, with "pardon for sin" among the blessings named.
- **Christ Our Hope in Life and Death** (Keith Getty / Matt Boswell / Jordan Kauflin / Matt Merker / Matt Papa, Getty, CCLI 7147502, graded Excellent / Prefer). Logged 8 times. It takes up the third verse's theme, the day "when my strength is failing," and grounds the singer's hope at death in Christ's death and resurrection.

**Not yet logged**

- **Praise, My Soul, the King of Heaven** (Henry Francis Lyte, 1834, hymn, public domain; no SongSelect number confirmed). Not in the catalog. Lyte's paraphrase of Psalm 103 puts the reasons in the first stanza: "ransomed, healed, restored, forgiven." Like the psalm, it does not name Christ, so pair it with a gospel song the way the psalm itself would be preached.

## Voice and addressee

The singer speaks as "I" and "my" throughout. The chorus speaks to the singer's own soul ("Bless the Lord, O my soul"), as Psalm 103 does, then turns at its last line to speak to God ("I'll worship Your holy name"). The verses address God as "You." There is no "we."

---

## How the text moves

The first verse starts at sunrise and asks to be singing at evening. The second verse gives the reasons: God's love, patience, greatness, kindness, and goodness. The third verse moves to the singer's death and promises praise "ten thousand years and then forevermore." The chorus repeats between verses without changing. The song moves through a lifetime, and the claim stays the same: the singer will keep praising God.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

The second verse is the only place the song says why God should be blessed: "You're rich in love and You're slow to anger." Without it the song is a resolution to keep singing, with nothing to sing about. The chorus line "Bless the Lord, O my soul" ties the song to Psalm 103:1, so it carries the song's only direct Scripture. No single line is false.

Scripture grounds that hope in Christ's resurrection (1 Corinthians 15:20–22). Here it rests only on the singer's resolve.

## Persons of the Trinity named

None is named. "The Lord" and "You" are God in general. Jesus, the Father as Father, and the Spirit are not named.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

Yes. A Jew could sing every line, since the chorus and second verse come from a psalm and nothing else in the lyric goes beyond it. A Muslim could sing almost all of it. The song never says anything only a Christian could believe, which is the clearest sign of its weakness in this church.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** "Sing like never before" and "ten thousand reasons for my heart to find" are about the singer's effort and discovery. "Your heart is kind" is true, but it is the song's own phrase.
- **Thin:** the whole lyric. The song promises reasons and gives a short list of attributes. The psalm it quotes gives more reasons in its first five verses than the song gives in all three.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 3

The lines that come from Psalm 103 are accurate. "Bless the Lord, O my soul" is Psalm 103:1, and "You're rich in love and You're slow to anger" is Psalm 103:8. "Let me be singing when the evening comes" fits Psalm 146:2, "I will praise the LORD as long as I live." Nothing in the lyric contradicts Scripture.

The score is 3 because the song takes the psalm's opening and drops its argument. Psalm 103 blesses the Lord for forgiving iniquity, redeeming life from the pit, and removing transgressions (103:3–4, 12). The song keeps the command to bless and replaces the reasons with the singer's promise to keep singing. Under the rubric, a short list of generic true statements is not unfolding Scripture.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 1

Christ is not in the song. There is no name, no title, no cross, no blood, no resurrection. Even the third verse's hope beyond death has no Christ in it. The song is a song of general praise to God. That is the reason for the 1.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 3

Talking to one's own soul is a biblical form, so the chorus is not merely subjective. The second verse is objective, a short list of what God is like. The first and third verses are about the singer: what he is doing at sunrise, what he hopes to be doing at evening, and what his soul will do when his strength fails. "Sing like never before" makes the singer's intensity part of the worship. Half the song is about God and half is about the singer's praise.

---

## Description of God's Character: 3

| Attribute | Line |
|---|---|
| Holiness | "Worship His holy name" |
| Love | "rich in love" |
| Patience | "slow to anger" |
| Greatness | "Your name is great" |
| Kindness, goodness | "Your heart is kind," "all Your goodness" |

The attributes are true, and "slow to anger" keeps a hint of God's justice. The list stays general. Sovereignty, justice, wisdom, faithfulness, and mercy to sinners are not named, and each attribute is one word.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 2

The song is easy to sing, and the congregation knows it well. Ease is not the measure here. Singing it teaches the people one psalm verse and a few attributes. They learn nothing of sin, Christ, or the gospel, and nothing that answers the "ten thousand reasons." The "I" voice and the stress on the singer's own singing make it less useful to a congregation that is meant to teach and admonish one another (Colossians 3:16).

---

## Tone

The tone is warm and steady. It builds through the verses to the long view of the third verse, and the chorus rises each time. Nothing in it is casual or sensual. The feeling is real, and the lyric gives it little to rest on.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never names Jesus, the cross, sin, forgiveness, or the resurrection, never sings God's justice, sovereignty, or faithfulness by name, never names the Father or the Spirit, never sings as "we," and never gives the specific reasons Psalm 103 gives for blessing the Lord.

---

## Setlist notes

This song is Weak. These notes are for the leader who still uses it now and then.

- **When it serves the sermon.** After a sermon on Psalm 103, Psalm 146, or Lamentations 3:22–23, where the reasons have already been preached. Even then, the Sovereign Grace *Bless the Lord, O My Soul (Psalm 103)* does the same work better.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** Everything in the gospel. Put it only after the Scripture reading and a song that names Christ and the cross, such as *It Was Finished Upon That Cross* or *Jesus, Thank You*.
- **Diet sentence.** It adds a short, warm resolve to praise God for life. It adds no gospel, so its place in the rotation should shrink.
- **What not to do.** Do not use it as the Psalm 103 song in a service on that psalm. Do not open with it before anything has been said about Christ. Do not repeat the chorus at length, since it is the part that teaches least.

## Identification notes

This is one song. Every setlist variant on file is a capitalization or key note, and "Bless the Lord (10000 Reasons)" is this song, not the Sovereign Grace Psalm 103 setting (`bless-the-lord-o-my-soul-psalm-103-sovereign-grace`).

The song is a co-write by Matt Redman and Jonas Myrin. Redman recorded and released it, which is why many people know it as his song.

The original 2011 copyright lines in several hymnals list SHOUT! Music Publishing, Hillsong's publishing company, as one of the publishers. That was most likely Jonas Myrin's share. Myrin began in the early 2000s as a worship leader at Hillsong Church London and co-wrote songs for Hillsong albums, and he has kept writing for Hillsong since, including "Cornerstone" (2012). Current PraiseCharts and Hymnary.org listings name Atlas Mountain Songs in place of SHOUT!, and Hillsong is no longer in the credits. Matt Redman recorded and released the song. The Avoid rule applies when Hillsong stands in the credits as the source of a song, so it does not apply here. The link is recorded so a leader who sees the old credit line knows it was checked. Myrin also co-wrote *The Lord Is My Salvation* (Getty), graded Excellent / Prefer, so his name alone is not a reason to avoid a song. The Weak label comes from the lyric.

---

## Why this overall label

The song opens with Psalm 103 and then leaves out what the psalm says. It names God's love and patience, promises lifelong and eternal praise, and never names Christ, sin, or the cross. The room can sing it easily, and it teaches little. That is Weak.
