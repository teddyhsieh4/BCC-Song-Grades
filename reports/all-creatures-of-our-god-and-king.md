## Thesis

All of creation, and then the saints, are summoned to praise the Creator. The hymn ends by naming the Father, the Son, and the Spirit.

---

## Overall recommendation

Strong

This is a clear, objective call to praise based on Psalm 148 and Francis’s Canticle of the Sun. The congregation sings true things about the Creator and, in the final stanza, names the Trinity. It does not preach the cross. Use it regularly as an opening or closing hymn of praise when the service has already put the gospel in the people’s mouths, or when the sermon is on creation, Psalm 148, or the glory of God in the works of His hands.

## Voice and addressee

The hymn addresses creation and the saints (“All creatures,” “Thou burning sun,” “And all ye saints of tender heart”). The final stanza addresses the congregation: “Let all things their creator bless.” It is corporate and objective.

---

## How the text moves

It begins with the sun, moon, wind, clouds, water, and fire, each summoned to praise. It then turns to the saints who forgive and who bear pain. It ends with the whole creation blessing the Creator and naming the Father, the Son, and the Spirit. The refrain (“O praise him… Alleluia”) does not change the claim. It keeps the same summons.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

The final stanza names the Trinity: “Praise, praise the Father, praise the Son / And praise the Spirit, Three in One.” Drop that and the hymn remains a true creation call but loses the explicit Trinitarian confession. The early stanzas are interchangeable in practice. The Trinity stanza is the one the grade depends on.

## Persons of the Trinity named

Father, Son, and Spirit are named in the final stanza. Earlier stanzas address the Creator without distinguishing the persons.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

A theist who believes in a Creator could sing the early stanzas. The final stanza names the Father, the Son, and the Spirit. That line forces the singer to the Christian confession. Drop the last stanza and a general theist could finish the hymn without correction.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** none. The images are drawn from the created order and from the ordinary sense of Psalm 148.
- **Thin:** the gospel. The hymn never names the cross or the finished work. That is expected in a creation hymn. It is still a gap the rest of the service must cover.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 5

The opening lines follow Psalm 148 and the Canticle of the Sun. “Thou burning sun with golden beam / Thou silver moon with softer gleam” is ordinary praise of the works of God’s hands. “Ye who long pain and sorrow bear / Praise God and on him cast your care” is 1 Peter 5:7 in congregational English. The final stanza is the ordinary Trinitarian confession. The text does not stretch Scripture.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 3

The Son is named in the final stanza. The hymn never says why the Son is to be praised. No cross, no substitution, no resurrection. A creation hymn that ends by naming the Trinity is stronger than a generic “God is great.” It is still not gospel-saturated.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 5

The hymn declares what creation and the saints are to do. It does not report the singer’s feelings. “Lift up your voice and with us sing” is a corporate summons, not a personal testimony.

---

## Description of God’s Character: 4

Creator, the one worthy of praise from every work of His hands, the one on whom the suffering cast their care, Father, Son, and Spirit. The attributes are not developed at length. Holiness and justice are not named. The hymn is still specific enough to teach.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 5

The language is plain. The tune is known. The congregation can sing it together without a weekly disclaimer. It teaches the scope of praise and, in the last stanza, the Trinity. That is useful here.

---

## Tone

Reverent, joyful, corporate. The repeated “Alleluia” and “O praise him” fit the summons.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never names its sin or the cross. The hymn never puts the finished work in the people’s mouth. That is the reason for pairing, not a reason to avoid the hymn.

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** Creation, Psalm 148, the glory of God in the works of His hands, or a service that has already named the gospel.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** The cross and the finished work of Christ.
- **Diet sentence.** This title adds a clear, objective call to praise the Creator and the Trinity. It never adds the gospel that the Creator has acted in Christ.
- **What not to do.** Do not drop the final stanza. Do not treat the early stanzas as sufficient by themselves.
