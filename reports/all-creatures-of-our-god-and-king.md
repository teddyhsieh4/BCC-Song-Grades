## Thesis

All of creation, and then the saints, are summoned to praise the Creator. The hymn ends by naming the Father, the Son, and the Spirit.

---

## Overall recommendation

Strong

Use it regularly as an opening or closing hymn of praise, especially when the sermon is on creation, Psalm 148, or God’s glory in what He has made. It is an objective call to praise drawn from Psalm 148 and Francis’s Canticle of the Sun, and its last stanza names the Trinity. It does not preach the cross, so let another song carry the gospel. The church has sung it 9 times across at least four years of the 2023–2026 window.

## Voice and addressee

The hymn speaks to creation and to the saints (“All creatures,” “Thou burning sun,” “And all ye saints of tender heart”). The last stanza turns to the congregation: “Let all things their creator bless.” The voice is corporate throughout.

---

## How the text moves

It summons sun, moon, wind, water, and fire to praise, then turns to the saints who forgive and who suffer. It ends with all creation blessing its Creator and naming the Father, the Son, and the Spirit. The refrain keeps the same summons from start to finish.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

The last stanza names the Trinity: “Praise, praise the Father, praise the Son / And praise the Spirit, Three in One.” Without it the hymn is still a true call to creation, but it loses its Christian confession. The earlier stanzas can be trimmed in practice. This one cannot.

## Persons of the Trinity named

Father, Son, and Spirit are named in the final stanza. Earlier stanzas address the Creator without distinguishing the persons.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

Partly. Any theist who believes in a Creator could sing the early stanzas. The last stanza’s Father, Son, and Spirit is what a general theist would have to change, so a version without it could be sung by almost anyone.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** none. The images come from the created order and from Psalm 148.
- **Thin:** the gospel. The hymn never names the cross or the finished work. That is expected in a creation hymn, and the rest of the service has to supply it.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 5

The opening lines follow Psalm 148 and the Canticle of the Sun. “Thou burning sun with golden beam / Thou silver moon with softer gleam” praises the works of God’s hands. “Ye who long pain and sorrow bear / Praise God and on him cast your care” is 1 Peter 5:7. The last stanza is the historic confession of the Trinity. No line stretches Scripture.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 3

The Son is named in the last stanza, but the hymn never says why He is to be praised. There is no cross, substitution, or resurrection. Ending on the Trinity puts it above a generic hymn to a Creator, and it scores 3.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 5

The hymn tells creation and the saints what to do. “Lift up your voice and with us sing” is a corporate summons, and no line reports the singer’s feelings.

---

## Description of God’s Character: 4

He is the Creator, worthy of praise from everything He made, the one on whom the suffering cast their cares, and Father, Son, and Spirit. These are not developed at length, and holiness and justice are not named, which is why this is a 4.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 5

The words are plain and the tune is well known. A congregation that sings it learns how wide God’s praise reaches and, in the last stanza, the Trinity. That earns a 5.

---

## Tone

Reverent and joyful. The repeated “Alleluia” and “O praise him” suit the summons.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never names its sin or the cross. Pair it with The Glory of the Cross when the service needs the finished work. The gap is a reason to pair the hymn, and no reason to avoid it.

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** Creation, Psalm 148, God’s glory in His works, or any service that has already named the gospel.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** The cross and the finished work of Christ.
- **Diet sentence.** Adds an objective call to praise the Creator, ending on the Trinity. It does not add the gospel of what the Creator has done in Christ.
- **What not to do.** Do not drop the last stanza.
