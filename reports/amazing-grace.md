# Amazing Grace

**Source:** John Newton, *Olney Hymns* (1779), Book I, Hymn 41, six stanzas, headed “Faith's Review and Expectation,” from 1 Chronicles 17:16-17. Text read 26 Nov 2026 from Hymnary's representative text and from the Cyber Hymnal printing of the same six stanzas. The later “ten thousand years” stanza is discussed under Identification. It is not part of the 1779 text this grade scores.

## Thesis

Grace saved a wretch. It taught the heart to fear and then relieved the fear. It has brought the singer through dangers and will lead him home. The Lord's word secures hope. He is shield and portion. When flesh fails, and when the earth gives way, the God who called the singer will be his forever.

---

## Overall recommendation

Acceptable with notes

Sing it, and do not let it be the gospel song of the morning. The church has logged it once in this window, in 2024. The stanzas that have to stay are the wretch, the fear, and “His word my hope secures.” Jesus is never named. The cross is never named. Pair it with a song that says the blood. If the chart has dropped Newton's later stanzas and kept only the opening and “ten thousand years,” put “His word my hope secures” back in, or the hour needs another song that sings the word of God.

## Voice and addressee

First person singular from the first line to the last. “A wretch like me,” “I once was lost,” “my heart,” “I first believed,” “I have already come,” “good to me,” “who called me.” The Lord and God are spoken about, not addressed as “You.” The church sings one person's review of grace, in Newton's own “I,” which a congregation can take as its own testimony.

---

## How the text moves

It opens with the sound of grace and the rescue of a wretch: lost, then found; blind, then seeing. The second stanza says grace taught fear and then relieved it, and names the hour of believing. The third looks back over dangers, toils, and snares, and forward to grace leading the singer home. The fourth rests hope on the Lord's promise and on his word, and calls him shield and portion for as long as life lasts. The fifth looks through death, “within the veil,” to joy and peace. The sixth looks through the end of the present world to the God who called the singer and will be his forever. The claim grows from conversion, to perseverance, to the word, to death, to the end. It never arrives at the name of Jesus.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

“Amazing grace! (how sweet the sound) / That saved a wretch like me” is the line the grade depends on, with “'Twas grace that taught my heart to fear” and “His word my hope secures.” “God, who called me here below, / Will be forever mine” is the other line that should not be dropped if the church is singing the 1779 text. “How sweet the sound,” by itself, is affection. “When we've been there ten thousand years” is not Newton's, and singing it in place of the word, the veil, and the calling would trade those lines for unending praise. No line in the six is false. Dropping “wretch” for a softer word would empty the first stanza. Dropping the word and the calling would leave a testimony of rescue with less of God in it.

## Persons of the Trinity named

“The Lord” and “God” are the names. “God, who called me” is the one who calls. Father, Son, and Spirit are unnamed. Grace is the actor of the first three stanzas, and the hymn does not say that this grace is the grace of the Son.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

Someone who will not say the name of Jesus can sing every stanza as it stands. “Grace,” “the Lord,” and “God, who called me” do not force the name. A moralist who thinks of grace as a mood will have trouble with “wretch,” with fear taught to the heart, and with a hope that rests on “his word.” Those lines are the friction. They are not the cross. A member of another religion who prays to another lord should stop at “The Lord” and at “God, who called me,” and many will not, because the hymn never says which Lord, or how he saved the wretch.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none in the 1779 text.
- **Non-biblical:** “How sweet the sound.” Sweetness is the singer's ear. The next line says what the grace did.
- **Thin:** “Through many dangers, toils and snares / I have already come.” That is true testimony, and it does not say what the dangers were or how grace held. “Will be forever mine” is the singer's claim on God. Read with “shield and portion,” it matches the God who is the singer's portion. Read alone, it is easy to sing as possession and stop.

The “ten thousand years” stanza, if the church adds it, is unending praise. The line does not contradict Scripture. Newton did not write it, and it does not supply the cross the six stanzas omit.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 4

“That saved a wretch like me” uses the word Paul uses in Romans 7:24, “Wretched man that I am!” Paul's next sentence asks who will deliver him and answers, “through Jesus Christ our Lord.” The hymn says grace saved the wretch and does not go on to that name. “I once was lost, but now am found” is the wording of Luke 15:24, “he was lost, and is found.” “Was blind, but now I see” takes up the words of John 9:25, “though I was blind, now I see,” and uses them for conversion. John 9 tells a healing and then a confession of faith. The hymn uses the pair as a figure for the rescue grace has already worked.

“'Twas grace that taught my heart to fear, / And grace my fears relieved” agrees with Psalm 130:4, “with you there is forgiveness, that you may be feared.” Fear and relief are both from God. “The hour I first believed” is the singer's date for faith. Ephesians 2:8 says salvation is by grace through faith. The hour is the singer's testimony. No verse dates it. “His word my hope secures” agrees with Psalm 119:49, the word in which God has made his servant hope. “He will my shield and portion be” joins Psalm 28:7, “The LORD is my strength and my shield,” and Psalm 73:26, “God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever.”

“Within the veil” is Hebrews 6:19, the hope that “enters into the inner place behind the curtain.” “The earth shall soon dissolve like snow” is Newton's picture. Second Peter 3:10 says the heavenly bodies will be dissolved and the earth laid bare. The snow is his. “God, who called me here below” agrees with Romans 8:30, “those whom he predestined he also called,” and with 1 Corinthians 1:9, the God by whom the church was called into the fellowship of his Son. The hymn says “called” and does not say “his Son.” The Olney heading, 1 Chronicles 17:16, is David's “Who am I.” Newton's hymn reviews grace from that place. The lines are accurate. The cross is not told.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 2

The person of Christ is absent. The finished work is absent. “Saved a wretch,” “lost” and “found,” and “the hour I first believed” are the fruit of the gospel in one life. Grace is the name of the cause. Blood, cross, resurrection, and the name Jesus are not in any of the six stanzas. “Within the veil” is priestly language with the priest unnamed. A church that knows the gospel will sing this as gospel. The lyric itself does not say it.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 3

The hymn is testimony. Almost every verb has “I” or “me” as its object or its subject: saved me, taught my heart, I believed, I have come, promised good to me, called me. What it testifies is what grace and the Lord have done. The room is not asked to produce a feeling on cue. “How sweet the sound” is affection in the opening line. “I will” never appears. There is no vow to be braver next week. The score stays at 3 because the song is one person's story, and the story is about God's grace.

---

## Description of God’s Character: 4

Grace saves, teaches fear, relieves fear, brings the singer through snares, and will lead him home. The Lord promises good. His word secures hope. He is shield and portion for the length of life. He is the God who calls and who remains when flesh fails and when the world gives way. Fear belongs with his holiness. The word and the promise belong with his faithfulness. Shield and portion belong with his care. Wrath is not named. A grace that teaches the heart to fear is still a holy grace. The cross is outside the hymn. The attributes that are here are sung as works: saving, teaching fear, securing hope, shielding, calling.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 4

Once in 2024 means this window has barely logged it. The sentences are plain. A congregation that sings the six stanzas learns that grace saves a wretch, that the same grace teaches fear and relieves it, that hope is secured by God's word, and that the God who calls keeps his people through death. They do not learn how the wretch was saved. “Wretch” should be sung as Newton wrote it. A softer noun would waste the first stanza. The fourth stanza is the one a band is most likely to skip, and it is the one that teaches the word, the shield, and the portion.

---

## Tone

The tone is astonished and plain. The line that sets it is “Amazing grace! (how sweet the sound) / That saved a wretch like me.” Sweetness and wretch are in the same breath. The later stanzas are quieter: dangers, the word, the failing of flesh, the earth dissolving. Nothing in the six is cute. The astonishment stays tied to a rescue.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never says the name of Jesus, never says that he died for sin and rose, and never says that the grace in the first line is the grace of his blood.

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** Ephesians 2:8, Luke 15, Psalm 130, or a testimony of calling, with the wretch and the fear left in. The fourth stanza belongs with a sermon on the word. The sixth belongs with a sermon on calling and the end.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** The name of Christ and the cross. Pair it with *Rock of Ages* (logged 6 times) or *Hallelujah, What a Savior* (logged 6 times) so “saved a wretch” is not left without the blood.
- **Diet sentence.** Once in this window, it adds a full testimony of saving, fearing, keeping grace, and of the word that secures hope. It never adds the cross.
- **What not to do.** Do not replace “wretch.” Do not drop “His word my hope secures,” “within the veil,” and “God, who called me” in order to make room for “ten thousand years.” Do not make this the only song in a service about the death of Christ.

## Identification notes

One hymn. John Newton, 1779, six stanzas in *Olney Hymns*. The catalog title, writer, and year match that text, and the catalog already separates it from *Amazing Grace (My Chains Are Gone)*. The tune in ordinary American use is New Britain. No arrangement year is on file. The setlist only says the key of C, so the stanza list on the church chart is unknown.

The catalog prints CCLI 22000. This pass could not find 22000 on a public SongSelect citation. A church sheet for a short text (Newton, Rees, and Excell, with the ten thousand years stanza) prints CCLI 22025. Two other public sheets print CCLI Song # 4755360, with the SongSelect terms line, for the public-domain Newton text in the four-stanza form that includes “ten thousand years.” *My Chains Are Gone* is a different number (CCLI 4768151 on one of those same sheets) and a different song. The header uses 4755360 because that is the number on the license lines found for the traditional text. SongSelect itself was not opened. The reviewer should confirm the number before anyone reports the song.

The stanza beginning “When we've been there ten thousand years” does not appear in *Olney Hymns*. American books later joined this wandering stanza to Newton's words, notably Edwin O. Excell's *Coronation Hymns* (1910). Some printings credit John P. Rees. This grade does not count it as Newton's. If it is sung, it says that praise in heaven does not run out. It still does not name Christ.

---

## Why this overall label

Newton's six stanzas tell the truth about a wretch saved by grace, about fear taught and relieved, about a hope secured by the word, and about the God who calls and keeps. They never say the name of Jesus or the cross. Sung with those stanzas intact, and with the blood somewhere else in the hour, the hymn is one the church can use. It should not be asked to preach the gospel by itself.
