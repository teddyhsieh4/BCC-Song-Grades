## Thesis

The church asks to seek God's face, to know his heart, and to feel his presence and acceptance, and then falls before the Sovereign Lord crying “holy, holy.” The second chorus asks Jesus to draw the singer close until the world fades, because the singer wants to worship and obey.

---

## Overall recommendation

Weak

Do not feature it. The church has logged the medley 4 times across at least three years in this window (2023, 2024, 2026). “Sovereign Lord / We cry holy, holy” and “I desire to worship and obey” are the lines that have to stay if it is sung at all. “Feel Your presence, acceptance” and “Let the world around me fade away” are the lines that should not be left running. Jesus is named. His death and resurrection are not. Use it only as a short prayer after a song that has already said the cross.

---

## Alternative song suggestions

The lyric is reaching for the face of God, for nearness, and for a life that worships and obeys. Three of these the church already sings. One is not in the catalog.

**Already sung**

**Jesus, Your Mercy** (Bob Kauflin / Jordan Kauflin / Nathan Stiff, Sovereign Grace, CCLI 7138116). Logged 14 times across at least four years (2023, 2024, 2025, 2026). Nearness is asked at the point of mercy, and the mercy has a cross in view.

**Before the Throne of God Above** (Charitie Lees Bancroft / Vikki Cook, hymn, CCLI 2306412). Logged 8 times across at least four years (2023, 2024, 2025, 2026). The singer draws near the throne because a great High Priest ever lives and pleads there, and the sinless Savior's death satisfied God's justice.

**Be Thou My Vision** (Irish hymn, translated by Mary E. Byrne, versified by Eleanor Hull, CCLI 30639). Logged 7 times across at least four years (2023, 2024, 2025, 2026). The desire is for the Lord as sight, wisdom, and ruler, through the length of the life.

**Not yet logged**

**Come, Ye Sinners, Poor and Needy** (Joseph Hart, hymn, CCLI 22111). Not in the catalog. This is the public-domain Hart text. “Jesus ready stands to save you,” and the singer answers, “I will arise and go to Jesus.” The drawing near has a Savior in it.

## Voice and addressee

Bilbrough's chorus is “we,” speaking to God: seek your face, know your heart, move among us, sovereign Lord. Founds's chorus is “me,” speaking to Jesus: draw me close, the world around me, I desire to worship and obey. The medley moves from a corporate ask to a private one. Both address God. Only the second chorus says which person.

---

## How the text moves

It starts with seeking God's face and knowing his heart, and with feeling presence and acceptance. It asks him to move among the people and reveal his power, and to show presence and acceptance again. Then the church falls at his feet and cries “holy, holy” to the Sovereign Lord. The second song changes the ask. Jesus is named. The singer wants to be drawn closer until the world fades, and ends with a desire to worship and obey. Nothing after the holy cry, and nothing in the Jesus chorus, tells a saving act. The medley moves from face and presence, to holiness, to a private nearness.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

“At Your feet we fall / Sovereign Lord / We cry holy, holy” and “For I desire to worship and obey” are the lines the grade depends on. “Jesus, draw me close” is what puts the name into the medley. “Feel Your presence, acceptance” and “Let the world around me fade away” are the lines that teach the least if the band repeats only those. If those two are looped and the holy cry and the desire to obey are dropped, the church is asking for a feeling and for the world to disappear. No line is a contradiction of a text. Without “holy, holy” and “obey,” the medley would have little left to teach.

## Persons of the Trinity named

Jesus is named in Founds's chorus, and he is “Lord.” Bilbrough's chorus says “Sovereign Lord” without saying Father, Son, or Spirit. The Spirit is unnamed. “Move among us now” asks for a presence and does not say whose.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

Bilbrough's half can be sung by anyone seeking a holy sovereign presence. “Feel Your presence, acceptance” does not require the gospel. Founds's half stops a worshiper of another god at the name Jesus. It does not stop someone who admires Jesus and wants to feel close to him. “Let the world around me fade away” can be sung as a mood. “I desire to worship and obey” is the line that asks for obedience, and it still does not say the cross, the blood, or the resurrection.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** “Feel Your presence, acceptance.” Presence and acceptance are asked for as feelings. No verse is cited by the line, and no sin is named that would make acceptance a matter of forgiveness. “Let the world around me fade away” asks for the world to leave the singer's attention. That is a mood.
- **Thin:** “May we know Your heart,” “Come reveal Your power,” and “Show Your presence.” The holy cry is the true line next to these. “Closer, Lord, to You” is a real desire, said with no means.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 3

“As we seek Your face” agrees with Psalm 27:8, “Your face, LORD, do I seek.” “At Your feet we fall / Sovereign Lord / We cry holy, holy” agrees with the seraphim in Isaiah 6:3, “Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts,” and with Revelation 4:8. The song cries the first two words and does not go on to “the whole earth is full of his glory” or “who was and is and is to come.” Falling at his feet fits Revelation 1:17, where John falls as though dead before the risen Christ. The chorus does not say that.

“Jesus, draw me close” is close to James 4:8, “Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you.” James's next words are “Cleanse your hands, you sinners.” The chorus has the nearness and, at the end, “worship and obey.” It does not have the sinners or the cleansing. “I desire to worship and obey” is a right desire. Founds's own story sheet for this chorus (rickfounds.com, “The story behind the song,” CCLI 443680) says he was reading Psalm 42 when he wrote it. Psalm 42 is a thirst for the living God in trouble. The lyric does not sing the trouble, the tears, or the “Why?”

“Feel Your presence, acceptance” and “Let the world around me fade away” cannot be tied to a text in its ordinary sense. The face, the holy cry, and the desire to obey can. The medley gives those three less time than the feelings.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 2

Jesus is named once, as the Lord who is asked to draw the singer close. There is no cross, no blood, no sin, no burial, and no resurrection. “Worship and obey” is lordship language with the saving work left out. Bilbrough's “Sovereign Lord” and “holy, holy” could be sung to God without ever reaching the Son. The medley exalts a near Jesus and leaves his finished work unsung.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 2

“Sovereign Lord” and “holy, holy” declare who he is. That cry is the objective center of Bilbrough's chorus. Around it, the verbs are “feel,” “know Your heart,” “reveal Your power,” and “show Your presence.” Founds's chorus is desire: “draw me close,” “let the world around me fade away,” “I desire.” “Worship and obey” aims the desire at something outside the feelings, and it is one line after the world has been asked to fade. The medley is mostly an ask to feel near.

---

## Description of God’s Character: 3

He is Sovereign Lord. He is the one whose name draws out “holy, holy.” He has a face the church seeks, a heart the church asks to know, and power he is asked to reveal. Jesus is Lord, and he is the one who can draw a sinner close. Holiness and sovereignty are the specific words. “Presence” and “acceptance” are the words the first chorus repeats, and they are asked for as experiences. Love, justice, wrath, and faithfulness are unnamed. The portrait is holiness at his feet, then a presence the singer wants to feel.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 3

Four logs across at least three years means the medley has been in the rotation often enough to be familiar, and not often enough to be a staple. The words are short and easy. A congregation can learn to seek God's face, to fall before a holy Sovereign, and to ask for a life that worships and obeys. They can also learn to ask to feel acceptance and to have the world fade. The second lesson is the one that will stick if the band loops it. “Holy, holy” and “obey” are the lines that teach. They are easy to skip because they are not the titles of either chorus.

---

## Tone

The tone is hushed longing. The line that sets it is “Let the world around me fade away,” after “Jesus, draw me close.” Bilbrough's “We cry holy, holy” is the sterner moment, and it is easy to sing it softly and pass on. Nothing here is playful. The nearness is the whole mood.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never says that Jesus died for sin and rose, never names a transgression, and never says that acceptance was purchased by his blood.

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** Psalm 27:8 or Isaiah 6, if “seek Your face” and “holy, holy” are actually sung. James 4:8 only if the sermon has already said “Cleanse your hands, you sinners.” The medley does not serve a sermon on the cross.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** The cross and the forgiveness that makes acceptance true. Pair it with *Jesus, Your Mercy* or *Before the Throne of God Above* so nearness is not left as a feeling.
- **Diet sentence.** Four times in at least three years, it adds a short prayer for God's face, a holy cry, and a desire to obey. It never adds the gospel.
- **What not to do.** Do not loop “feel Your presence, acceptance” or “fade away.” Do not drop “holy, holy” or “worship and obey.” Do not use the medley as the call to worship on a Sunday when no other song names the cross.

## Why this overall label

The medley asks the church to seek God's face, to cry “holy” at the feet of the Sovereign Lord, and to desire obedience to Jesus. It also asks them to feel presence and acceptance, and to have the world fade, and it never says the cross. Four uses in three years is already more than the lyric can carry. It should stay short, and it should not open the service.
