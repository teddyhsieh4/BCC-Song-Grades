## Thesis

The chorus says that Christ in the singer is life and that death is gain, and then calls Christ King, song, life, joy, strength, sword, peace, and Lord.

---

## Overall recommendation

Weak

Keep this chorus off a feature spot. The church has logged it once in the 2023–Sept 2026 window, in 2024. If it is used, sing it only after Philippians 1 has been read, and do not cut the opening line. The reason for Weak is the thinness of the whole text. “Christ in me is to live, to die is to gain” takes up Philippians 1:21 with the first clause turned around and “in me” added, and the rest of the chorus is a list of “He’s my” titles, including King, Lord, life, and peace. The lyric never says how Christ came to be in the singer, and it never says the cross, the resurrection, or what the gain of death is.

---

## Alternative song suggestions

The theme in view is Philippians 1:21: Christ as the Christian’s life, death as gain, and Christ confessed as Lord. Two of these the church already sings. One is not in the catalog.

**Already sung**

**Yet Not I but Through Christ in Me** (Jonny Robinson / Rich Thompson / Michael Farren, CityAlight, CCLI 7121852). Logged 10 times across at least four years (2023, 2024, 2025, 2026). The life the church sings is Christ’s. The third verse states the blood, the pardon, and the resurrection, and the refrain keeps that life in Him.

**Christ Our Hope in Life and Death** (Keith Getty / Matt Boswell / Jordan Kauflin / Matt Merker / Matt Papa, Getty, CCLI 7147502). Logged 8 times across at least four years (2023, 2024, 2025, 2026). It asks what hope we have in living and in dying, and it answers with the Redeemer’s blood and the Lord who will raise His people.

**Not yet logged**

**To Live Is Christ** (Mark Altrogge / Bob Kauflin, Sovereign Grace, CCLI 5937857, © 2011 Sovereign Grace Praise, from *Risen*). Not in the catalog. The chorus sings Paul’s line in his order: “To live is Christ, to die is gain.” The verses confess that God chose a people to be saved, that Jesus rose and will raise His own, and that the Lamb who was slain will bring them to His side.

## Voice and addressee

One singer speaks, in the singular (“me,” “my”), about Christ in the third person (“He’s”). The singer does not pray to the Father or the Son, and does not exhort the room. The congregation takes up that person’s words.

---

## How the text moves

The published text is four lines. It states the life-and-death line, repeats it, and then lists the titles. Nothing after the repeat changes the claim. There is no later stanza that adds a cross, a resurrection, or a reason that death is gain.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

The opening line is the one tied to a text: “Christ in me is to live, to die is to gain.” This chorus has no second stanza behind it. If a band drops that line and keeps “He’s my King, He’s my song, He’s my life, He’s my joy” with the lines on strength, sword, peace, and Lord, the room is left with personal titles and no verse under them. “He’s my sword” is the loosest image in the list. Removing it would still leave a chorus with no cross and no resurrection. No one line is false, and no single line would change the label on its own. If the chorus is sung at all, the opening line has to stay.

## Persons of the Trinity named

The lyric names Christ, then calls Him King and Lord. The Father and the Spirit are unnamed, the word Son never appears, and the name Jesus is never sung. “Christ in me” speaks of the Son’s indwelling under the title Christ. Romans 8:9-10 holds the Spirit’s dwelling and “Christ is in you” together. This chorus names only Christ.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

A member of another religion cannot sing the chorus as written. The first line names Christ, and the last calls Him Lord. Someone who admires Jesus and has not trusted Him could sing “He’s my joy” and “He’s my peace,” because those lines never say what He did. The line that would stop that singer is “to die is to gain,” which treats death as profit. If the band drops the opening and stays on “He’s my joy” and “He’s my peace,” the name Christ drops out of the song, and the “He” in those lines is no longer identified.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** none.
- **Thin:** “Christ in me is to live, to die is to gain.” The line uses Philippians 1:21 and leaves Paul’s argument around that verse unsaid. “He’s my life,” “He’s my peace,” and “He’s my sword” take words from Colossians 3:4 (“Christ who is your life”), Ephesians 2:14 (“he himself is our peace”), and Deuteronomy 33:29 (“the sword of your triumph”), and leave the rest of each verse unsaid. Song, joy, and strength are true words with the saving acts left out.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 3

“Christ in me is to live, to die is to gain.” Philippians 1:21 reads, “For to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain.” Paul says that living, for him, is Christ, and that dying is gain. The chorus turns the first clause around and adds “in me.” Indwelling is a real doctrine beside that verse. Galatians 2:20 says “Christ who lives in me,” and that the Son of God “loved me and gave himself for me.” Colossians 1:27 calls the mystery “Christ in you, the hope of glory.” The chorus keeps the indwelling and the claim that death is gain, and it leaves out “gave himself for me” and “the hope of glory.”

“He’s my strength” and “He’s my song” stand near Isaiah 12:2, “the LORD GOD is my strength and my song, and he has become my salvation.” The chorus splits strength and song into two titles and does not sing the salvation. “He’s my King” and “He’s my Lord” are true titles. Philippians 2:9-11 says that God “has highly exalted him,” and that “every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord.” This lyric uses the titles and does not say the exaltation.

“He’s my life” says that Christ is the singer’s life. Colossians 3:4 reads, “When Christ who is your life appears, then you also will appear with him in glory.” The chorus keeps the title and does not say the appearing. Ephesians 2:14 says “he himself is our peace, who has made us both one and has broken down in his flesh the dividing wall of hostility.” “He’s my peace” uses that title and does not say that he made the two one. Deuteronomy 33:29 calls the LORD “the sword of your triumph” for “a people saved by the LORD.” “He’s my sword” uses the title and does not say that the people are saved by the LORD.

The opening line can be tied to Philippians 1:21, and nothing in the four lines contradicts that verse. The titles are true, and they leave Philippians 1 untaught.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 2

Christ is the subject of every line. The singer names Him, calls Him King and Lord, and says that life and death have to do with Him. The same singer can finish the chorus without having said that Christ died or rose. “To die is to gain” speaks of a hope and does not say that the gain is to depart and be with Christ, which is Philippians 1:23. A name and an office, with that work left unsaid, is a 2 here.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 3

The opening claim already includes the singer: “Christ in me.” The second half is eight titles in a row, each one “my.” “He’s my King” and “He’s my Lord” name real offices. “He’s my life” and “He’s my peace” are titles Scripture gives Christ (Colossians 3:4; Ephesians 2:14). There is no “I will” vow. The chorus gives no account of Christ’s work under those titles. The offices and titles are true, and the singer’s “my” is on every one of them. True but thin: the score is 3.

---

## Description of God’s Character: 3

The offices in the lyric are King and Lord. Six of the eight titles describe Christ by what He is for the singer: song, life, joy, strength, sword, and peace. Life and peace are words for Christ in Colossians 3:4, “Christ who is your life,” and Ephesians 2:14, “he himself is our peace.” Sword is a word for the LORD in Deuteronomy 33:29, “the sword of your triumph.” Holiness, justice, mercy, wrath, faithfulness, and love are unnamed. King is not given a throne or a reign. The titles are true and thin, because the chorus gives no account of Christ’s work. The score is 3.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 3

King, Lord, life, joy, peace, and strength are words the room already knows, and the church has sung the chorus once in this window, in 2024, in D. What the people learn is the compressed Philippians line and a list of titles. They do not learn why death is gain, or what Christ did so that He could be called their life. The voice is “me,” so each person in the room sings the claim in the singular. It can follow a reading. It will not teach Philippians 1 by itself.

---

## Tone

The tone is earnest and personal. The line that sets it is “He’s my joy / He’s my strength, He’s my sword, He’s my peace, He’s my Lord.” “He’s my sword” puts a hard word in the same gentle list as “He’s my peace,” and the chorus never explains the word.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never confesses sin, the cross, the resurrection, the Spirit, repentance, or the hope of departing to be with Christ.

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** Philippians 1:21-23, and only after that passage has been read. A Galatians 2:20 sermon can use it as a brief response if the reading has already included “gave himself for me,” since the chorus will not say that. Colossians 3:3-4, where Christ is our life, fits only when the sermon has said that we died with Him.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** The cross, the resurrection, and the plain sense of “to die is gain,” which is to depart and be with Christ. Pair it with *Yet Not I but Through Christ in Me* when the hour still needs the blood and the resurrection, and with *Christ Our Hope in Life and Death* when the sermon is living and dying.
- **Diet sentence.** Across a year it can add one short confession that Christ is the believer’s life and Lord. One log in 2024 means it has not shaped the diet. It never adds the cross, repentance, or the resurrection.
- **What not to do.** Do not drop the opening line and loop “He’s my joy” and “He’s my peace.” Do not give this chorus the gospel spot in the service. There is no richer stanza to protect. If it is sung, keep “Christ in me is to live, to die is to gain,” and let the next song state the cross and the resurrection.

## Why this overall label

The chorus gives the church true titles for Christ, King and Lord among them, and a recognizable line from Philippians 1:21. The chorus never says the cross, the resurrection, or the reason Paul gives for the gain. In Philippians 1:23 the gain is to depart and be with Christ, and this chorus never says that.
