## Thesis

Amazing grace saved a wretch, lost and found, blind and now seeing. Christ is risen from the grave. The prodigal is welcomed home, the sinner is now a saint, because the God who died came back to life. Death’s sting and fear’s power are answered by the King of kings, who has disarmed them. The singer looks to scars, open arms, and a face on the day of the call.

## Voice and addressee

The first stanza is testimony in the singular, the Newton lines: “saved a wretch like me.” The chorus is corporate praise: “Hallelujah, Christ is risen from the grave.” “The prodigal is welcomed home” and “the sinner now a saint” speak about those people. “Oh death, where is your sting” addresses death. The last stanza turns to Jesus as “You”: “the day You call me,” “I’ll see Your scars.” The church would be singing a personal rescue, a shared hallelujah, and a last look at his face.

---

## How the text moves

It starts with Newton’s grace: a wretch saved, lost then found, blind then seeing. The chorus answers with one fact, repeated: Christ is risen from the grave. The next stanza welcomes the prodigal home, calls the sinner a saint, and gives the reason: the God who died came back to life, and everything has changed. A bridge asks death where its sting is and fear where its power is, and says the King of kings has disarmed them. The people are delivered and redeemed, and eternal life is theirs. The last stanza looks to the day of the call, scars, open arms, and the beauty of his face, and to praise that lasts. The chorus does not add a new doctrine. The death and the rising are the center. The ending is the singer’s sight of the scars.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

“Hallelujah, Christ is risen from the grave” is the line the grade of the words depends on, with “The God who died came back to life” and “Delivered and redeemed.” “Amazing grace, how sweet the sound / That saved a wretch like me” is Newton’s confession and belongs with the resurrection, or the rising is left without the wretch. “I’ll see Your scars” is the line that shows the rising one still bears the marks. “Heaven’s sweet embrace” is the softer ending. If the band keeps the embrace and the hallelujah and drops the death, the redeeming, and the scars, the resurrection is a shout without the cross behind it. No line in the lyric is the reason for Avoid. The credit is.

## Persons of the Trinity named

Christ is named, risen from the grave, the God who died and came back to life, the King of kings, the one whose scars and face the singer will see. The Father and the Spirit are unnamed. “Amazing grace” is not tied to a named person until the chorus names Christ.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

No. “Christ is risen from the grave,” “the God who died came back to life,” and “I’ll see Your scars” cannot be moved to another lord. “Amazing grace, how sweet the sound / That saved a wretch like me” can be sung by someone who means a vague mercy, which is why that stanza needs the resurrection chorus with it. The chorus stops the vagueness.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none in the lyric.
- **Non-biblical:** “Heaven’s sweet embrace” and “the beauty of Your face” as the last day’s main picture. The scars in the same stanza are the more specific hope.
- **Thin:** “And everything has changed.” The change is real if the death and the rising are kept. The line itself does not say what changed. “Oh fear, where is your power” extends the sting-of-death question to fear. Fear is not the subject of 1 Corinthians 15:55.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 4

“Amazing grace, how sweet the sound / That saved a wretch like me / I once was lost, but now I’m found / Was blind, but now I see” is Newton’s stanza. “Lost” and “found” agree with Luke 15:24. “Christ is risen from the grave” is the Easter confession. “The prodigal is welcomed home” is Luke 15. “The sinner now a saint” agrees with the call to be saints. “The God who died came back to life” says the death and the resurrection in one line. “Oh death, where is your sting” agrees with 1 Corinthians 15:55, “O death, where is your sting?” “The mighty King of kings has disarmed you” aims Colossians 2:15, where Christ disarmed the rulers, at death and at fear. The sting line is the text. The disarming of fear is the song’s extension. “Delivered and redeemed” and “I’ll see Your scars” agree with a risen Lord who still bears the marks, as Thomas sees the hands in John 20:27.

“Everything has changed” and “Heaven’s sweet embrace” are looser than those lines. The death, the grave, the sting, the redeeming, and the scars can be tied to texts. The score is 4 because the song is a true Easter confession with a soft ending and one broad line about everything changing.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 4

Christ died and is risen from the grave. A wretch is saved. A prodigal comes home. A sinner is called a saint. The people are delivered and redeemed. The scars remain on the one who lives. The word cross is absent, and wrath is absent. The death and the rising are explicit, which keeps this out of the range of a title-only song. The blood and the bearing of sin are not spelled out. The finished work is the death and the resurrection, sung clearly, with the cost left in the scars and in Newton’s wretch.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 3

“Christ is risen from the grave,” “the God who died came back to life,” and “death, where is your sting” declare what God has done. “Saved a wretch like me,” “now I’m found,” “the day You call me,” and “I’ll see Your scars, Your open arms, the beauty of Your face” are the singer’s story and the singer’s hope of sight. The hallelujah is corporate. The testimony and the embrace take a real share of the song. The score is 3 because the facts are strong and the last stanza is the singer’s meeting.

---

## Description of God’s Character: 3

Grace saves a wretch. Christ rises. The King of kings disarms death. He welcomes the prodigal, makes a sinner a saint, delivers, and redeems. He bears scars and will show his face. Love is in the welcome and in “Heaven’s sweet embrace.” Holiness and wrath are unnamed. The rising King is specific. The ending leans on sweetness and beauty. The portrait is grace, kingship, and a risen Lord with scars, and it does not go on to his justice.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 4

Five logs across at least four years means the room already knows the chorus. On the words alone, a congregation can learn that Christ died and rose, that grace saves a wretch, that death’s sting is answered, and that the risen one still has scars. “Hallelujah, Christ is risen from the grave” is plain. “Heaven’s sweet embrace” is the line that softens the last day. The lyric would be usable at Easter if the credit were one this church sings. The credit is the reason it is not used. The usefulness score is about the words.

---

## Tone

The tone is glad and, at the end, tender. The line that sets the gladness is “Hallelujah, Christ is risen from the grave.” “Oh death, where is your sting” is the challenge under the gladness. “Heaven’s sweet embrace” and “the beauty of Your face” set the last stanza’s tenderness. The scars keep that tenderness attached to the wounds.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never says the word cross, never says that wrath was borne, and never names the Spirit.

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** It does not. The lyric would fit Easter, 1 Corinthians 15, or Luke 15. The credit keeps it out of the service.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** Use another resurrection text. *Christ Our Hope in Life and Death* and *Yet Not I but Through Christ in Me* cover the grave and the hope without this credit.
- **Diet sentence.** Five times in at least four years, the lyric would have added a plain “Christ is risen” and Newton’s wretch. The credit means it adds nothing the church should keep singing.
- **What not to do.** Do not keep it because the chorus is easy or because the room already knows it. Do not drop the Bethel credit and sing the words anyway.
