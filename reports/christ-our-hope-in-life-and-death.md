## Thesis

The only hope and confidence in life and death is Christ alone: the Redeemer whose blood makes God’s goodness known, the Rock who holds faith in trial, and the living Lord who will raise his people and destroy sin and death.

---

## Overall recommendation

Excellent / Prefer

Feature it freely. The three verses move from belonging to Christ, through the blood that grounds God’s goodness, to the grave that is answered by the living Christ and the feast that follows. The chorus confesses the same claim. The text is dense enough to carry a service on 1 Corinthians 15, Romans 14:7–9, or the Heidelberg Catechism’s first question. It has already been logged in at least four years of the current window.

## Voice and addressee

First-person plural (“our hope,” “our souls,” “we confess”). The questions are answered by the congregation. The chorus addresses the room to sing and confess. The voice is corporate.

---

## How the text moves

It opens with the Heidelberg question and answers it with belonging to Christ and the love that keeps to the end. The second verse grounds God’s goodness in the Redeemer’s blood and names Christ as the Rock above the trial. The third verse faces the grave, answers it with the living Christ, and ends with the resurrection feast. The chorus returns the same confession after each verse.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

The second verse (“Where is his grace and goodness known? / In our great Redeemer’s blood”) and the third (“Unto the grave, what will we sing? / ‘Christ, he lives; Christ, he lives!’ / … Then sin and death will be destroyed”). Drop either and the hope loses its ground in the finished work and the resurrection. The first verse alone still names Christ as hope but leaves the blood and the empty grave unsaid.

## Persons of the Trinity named

Christ is named throughout as hope, Redeemer, Rock, and the living Lord. God is named as good and as the one whose command holds the days. The Spirit is not named.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

No. The hope is Christ alone, the goodness is known in the Redeemer’s blood, and the grave is answered by the living Christ who destroys sin and death. A vague theist cannot sing the second and third verses without correction.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** none that affect the claim.
- **Thin:** “God is good, God is good” is true and underfed until the next line places it in the Redeemer’s blood. The rest of the text is specific.

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 5

“What is our hope in life and death? / Christ alone” and “our souls to him belong” follow the ordinary sense of Romans 14:7–9 and the Heidelberg answer that the soul belongs to the faithful Savior. “In our great Redeemer’s blood” states the ground of grace. “Christ, he lives” and “we will rise to meet the Lord / Then sin and death will be destroyed” follow 1 Corinthians 15:20–26 and 1 Thessalonians 4:16–17. The ordinary sense of the texts is in view.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 5

Christ is the only hope, the Redeemer whose blood makes goodness known, the Rock who holds faith, and the living Lord who raises his people and ends sin and death. The blood and the resurrection are stated. Titles are joined to the finished work.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 5

The voice is corporate and confessional. “Our hope,” “our souls to him belong,” and “we confess” declare what is true of the people of Christ. The questions are answered by objective claims, not by reports of inner weather.

---

## Description of God’s Character: 4

God is good, and that goodness is known in the Redeemer’s blood. Christ holds the days, stands above the trial, and is the Rock. The attributes are specific once the blood is named. The double “God is good” is thin by itself; the following line supplies the ground.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 5

The diction is plain. The argument is complete in three verses and a chorus. The congregation has already sung it in multiple years. The corporate voice and the repeated confession make it useful for teaching belonging, the blood, and the resurrection.

---

## Tone

Reverent. The language stays with hope, the Redeemer’s blood, the Rock, the grave, and the living Christ. It does not ask the room to confer on God what He already possesses.

---

## What the congregation never says

The Spirit’s work is not named. Explicit substitutionary language beyond “Redeemer’s blood” is not expanded. Wrath is not named. Pair the song with texts or songs that name the Spirit or the satisfaction of justice when the sermon of the day requires them.

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** 1 Corinthians 15 (resurrection, death destroyed), Romans 14:7–9 (belonging to the Lord in life and death), Hebrews 9–10 (blood), or any text on the only comfort in life and death.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** Explicit naming of the Spirit if the sermon requires it; a fuller statement of propitiation if the text of the day is Romans 3 or the cross.
- **Diet sentence.** Adds a dense, corporate confession of belonging to Christ, the blood that grounds God’s goodness, and the resurrection hope that faces the grave. It does not add a Spirit-focused or purely penitential song.
- **What not to do.** Do not drop the second or third verse. Do not tag it with a vague chorus that flattens the blood and the empty grave into generic hope.
