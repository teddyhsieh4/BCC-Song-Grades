## Thesis

God has been God from everlasting. Our days are few and held in His hands. By His grace He is making all things new, so we rest in the wisdom of His plan while we number our days.

---

## Overall recommendation

Strong

Use it regularly when the sermon or the service needs the congregation to face the shortness of life under the everlasting God. The final verse is the one that must stay: it asks God to satisfy us in our numbered days and to establish the work of our hands while we wait. The lyric is faithful to Psalm 90 and clear, but it does not name Christ or the cross. Pair it with a song that does.

## Voice and addressee

The song speaks to God (“O God”) and about God. The voice is corporate “we” in the second verse and the chorus. It is prayer and declaration, not testimony about the singer’s inner state.

---

## How the text moves

It begins with God before creation and ends with the same confession: “From everlasting, You are God.” The middle verses move from the brevity of human life to the request that God would satisfy us in our numbered days and establish the work of our hands. The chorus keeps the same claim: our days are held, His favor has no end, we rest in His plan.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

“So satisfy us in our numbered days / Establish every effort while we wait / From everlasting, You are God.”

If the band drops the fourth verse, the song still declares God’s eternality and our frailty, but it loses the prayer that Psalm 90 itself makes: teach us to number our days that we may gain a heart of wisdom (Ps 90:12). The chorus alone does not carry that request.

## Persons of the Trinity named

God is addressed and confessed. “O God of light” and “by Your grace You’re making all things new” stay within the language of the one God of Psalm 90 and Revelation 21. Father, Son, and Spirit are not distinguished.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

A theist who believes in an everlasting God who holds history could sing the whole song without correction. The name of Jesus is not present to swap. A moralist could affirm the shortness of life and the call to live wisely. The final verse’s request for grace and satisfaction in numbered days still assumes the God of Scripture, but the lyric does not force a trinitarian or Christological confession.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** none that matter. The images of spring, summer, stars, and ancient skies stay inside the psalm’s contrast between God’s eternity and the created order.
- **Thin:** the gospel. The lyric never says why the everlasting God can receive frail sinners or why His favor has no end toward us. “By Your grace You’re making all things new” is true (Rev 21:5), but it is not explained.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 5

“O God, before the mountains were brought forth / Or days of spring and summer filled the earth / From everlasting, You are God” is Psalm 90:2 in ordinary sense. “A thousand years are nothing in Your sight” is Psalm 90:4. “Life is but a sigh” is the same note as Psalm 90:9–10 and James 4:14. “Satisfy us in our numbered days / Establish every effort while we wait” is the prayer of Psalm 90:12, 14, 17. The lines stay inside the text they name.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 3

Christ is not named. The cross is not named. The resurrection is not named. “By Your grace You’re making all things new” points forward to the new creation, but the lyric does not say how that grace came or who secured it. The song exalts the everlasting God of Psalm 90. It does not preach the finished work of Christ. That is why the score stays at 3.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 5

The lyric declares what is true of God and of our days. “We rest within the wisdom of Your plan” is a confession, not a report of feeling. There is no vow the singer must specially mean, and no move from lament into a private sense of arrival. The song stays with the psalm’s prayer.

---

## Description of God’s Character: 4

God is everlasting, before the mountains, the one in whose sight a thousand years are nothing. His love and favor have no end. He holds our days. He has a plan in which we can rest. He is the God of light who knows our ways. By grace He is making all things new. Holiness, justice, and wrath are not in view. The attributes that are named are specific and tied to the psalm rather than generic “great / good / love.”

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 4

The language is plain. The voice is corporate where it needs to be. The congregation can say these lines together without special explanation beyond the psalm itself. Singability is high, but that is not why the score is 4. The score is 4 because the lyric teaches the shortness of life under the everlasting God and then asks for wisdom and established work. It does not teach the cross. That limit is the reason for the notes, not a reason to call the song unclear.

---

## Tone

Reverent and measured. It does not ask the room to confer on God anything He does not already possess. The intensity stays inside the prayer of Psalm 90.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never says that Christ died for sins, that He rose, that His righteousness is counted to us, or that we repent and believe. Those confessions have to come from the rest of the service.

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** Psalm 90, Ecclesiastes, James 4, or any text that faces the brevity of life and the call to number our days. Also useful near the end of a service that has already preached the cross and now needs the congregation to rest in the God who holds the future.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** The name of Jesus, substitution, the cross, the resurrection, and the reason grace can make all things new.
- **Diet sentence.** It adds a clear, corporate confession of God’s eternality and the shortness of our days. It never adds the gospel of the crucified and risen Christ.
- **What not to do.** Do not let it stand as the only substantial song in a service. Do not drop the fourth verse. Do not treat the chorus as a soft landing that replaces the prayer of the psalm.

## Why this overall label

The lyric is a faithful, congregational setting of Psalm 90. It teaches what the psalm teaches and asks what the psalm asks. It does not name Christ or the finished work. That keeps it at Strong rather than Excellent / Prefer. Use it when the service needs the everlasting God and the numbered days; supply the cross elsewhere.
