## Thesis

God has been God from everlasting. Our days are few and held in His hands. By His grace He is making all things new, so we rest in the wisdom of His plan while we number our days.

---

## Overall recommendation

Strong

Use it regularly when the sermon or the service calls the congregation to face the shortness of life under the everlasting God. Keep the final verse, which asks God to satisfy us in our numbered days and establish the work of our hands. The lyric is faithful to Psalm 90 but never names Christ or the cross, so pair it with a song that does. The church has sung it 11 times across at least four years of the 2023–2026 window.

## Voice and addressee

The song speaks to God (“O God”) and about Him. The second verse and the chorus use a corporate “we.” It is prayer and declaration rather than testimony about how the singer feels.

---

## How the text moves

It opens and closes with God before creation: “From everlasting, You are God.” The middle verses turn from the brevity of human life to the prayer that God would satisfy us and establish our work. The chorus holds the same claim throughout: our days are in His hands and His favor has no end.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

“So satisfy us in our numbered days / Establish every effort while we wait / From everlasting, You are God.”

Without the fourth verse, the song still declares God’s eternity and our frailty, but it drops the prayer Psalm 90 itself makes: teach us to number our days that we may gain a heart of wisdom (Ps 90:12). The chorus does not make that request.

## Persons of the Trinity named

God is addressed and confessed. “O God of light” and “by Your grace You’re making all things new” use the language of Psalm 90 and Revelation 21. Father, Son, and Spirit are not named separately.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

Yes. Any theist who believes in an everlasting God who rules history could sing it as written, since the name of Jesus is never used. A moralist could also affirm the shortness of life and the call to live wisely. The prayer for grace in the last verse assumes the God of Scripture, but nothing in the lyric requires confessing the Trinity or Christ.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** none that affect the claim. Spring, summer, stars, and ancient skies serve the psalm’s contrast between God’s eternity and the created order.
- **Thin:** the gospel. The lyric never says why the everlasting God can receive frail sinners or why His favor toward us has no end. “By Your grace You’re making all things new” is true (Rev 21:5), but the song does not say how that grace came.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 5

“O God, before the mountains were brought forth / Or days of spring and summer filled the earth / From everlasting, You are God” is Psalm 90:2. “A thousand years are nothing in Your sight” is Psalm 90:4. “Life is but a sigh” echoes Psalm 90:9–10 and James 4:14. “Satisfy us in our numbered days / Establish every effort while we wait” is the prayer of Psalm 90:12, 14, 17.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 3

Christ, the cross, and the resurrection are not named. “By Your grace You’re making all things new” looks ahead to the new creation, but the lyric does not say who secured that grace. The song exalts the everlasting God of Psalm 90 without preaching the finished work, so it stays at 3.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 5

The lyric declares what is true of God and of our days. “We rest within the wisdom of Your plan” is a confession, not a report of a feeling, and the singer makes no vow. The song stays with the psalm’s prayer from start to finish.

---

## Description of God’s Character: 4

God is everlasting, older than the mountains, the one for whom a thousand years are nothing. His love and favor have no end, He holds our days, and His plan is wise enough to rest in. He is the God of light who knows our ways and is making all things new. Holiness, justice, and wrath are not in view, which is why this is a 4.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 4

The words are plain and the voice is corporate where it should be, so a congregation can sing it with no more explanation than the psalm itself. It teaches the shortness of life under the everlasting God and the prayer for wisdom. It does not teach the cross, and that is why the score is 4.

---

## Tone

Reverent and measured, the sober quiet of Psalm 90 itself.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never says that Christ died for sins, that He rose, that His righteousness is counted to us, or that we repent and believe. When the service needs those, pair it with In Christ Alone.

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** Psalm 90, Ecclesiastes, James 4, or any text on the brevity of life and numbering our days. It also works near the end of a service that has already preached the cross.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** The name of Jesus, substitution, the cross, the resurrection, and the reason grace can make all things new.
- **Diet sentence.** Adds a clear, corporate confession of God’s eternity and the shortness of our days. It never adds the gospel of the crucified and risen Christ.
- **What not to do.** Do not make it the only substantial song in a service, and do not drop the fourth verse.
