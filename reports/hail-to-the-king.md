## Thesis

Jesus came as a man while he was God, was pierced for our iniquities, and was exalted, so that every tongue will confess him Lord, and the church hails him until he comes and rival thrones fall.

---

## Overall recommendation

Strong

Use it when the sermon is Philippians 2 or Isaiah 53, and sing verse 1. The church has logged it three times in this window, in 2024 and 2025. The chorus, “Hail to the King / In all His splendor and majesty,” is praise. The doctrine is in the verses. “And gave to You the right to bear the name above all names” says what Philippians 2:9 says, that God “bestowed on him the name that is above every name.” Keep the cross line in the service.

## Voice and addressee

The verses and the pre-chorus speak to Christ (“You”). The chorus speaks about him (“Hail to the King,” “His splendor”). “We should bow” and “We will sing” are the church. The last line of the second verse names “the true and living God.”

---

## How the text moves

It begins with the incarnation and the cross, then the exaltation and the name. The church answers that at the name of Jesus every tongue will confess him Lord, and that his coming will draw the same song. The chorus hails him as King. The second verse waits for the risen King, the fall of rival thrones, and his authority. The claim moves from the cross already accomplished to the reign still ahead.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

“Pierced for our iniquities as You hung upon the cross” is the line the gospel in this song depends on. The chorus can be hailed over almost any king. Without the piercing and the cross, and without “in very nature God,” the song is coronation language. The name line belongs with the exaltation. Cutting it would not remove the cross. Dropping verse 1 would.

## Persons of the Trinity named

Jesus is named, and he is called Lord and God. “But God exalted You” speaks of God exalting Jesus, which is the Father’s act in Philippians 2:9, though the song does not say “Father.” The Spirit is unnamed.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

No. “In very nature God,” “hung upon the cross,” and “Lord Jesus our God” cannot be moved to another lord. The chorus alone, “Hail to the King / In all His splendor and majesty,” could be aimed at a different king if the verses are not sung. The verses are what make the hail mean Jesus.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** none.
- **Thin:** “In all His splendor and majesty” states glory without a scene. “Hail supreme authority in the true and living God” is a true line said once, at the end of the waiting verse.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 4

“You came to us a man in very nature God” confesses Philippians 2:6-8. The ESV says he was “in the form of God” and was “born in the likeness of men.” The song’s “very nature” is the same confession of deity and humanity in a different wording. “Pierced for our iniquities as You hung upon the cross” joins Isaiah 53:5, “he was pierced for our transgressions” and “crushed for our iniquities,” with the cross. “But God exalted You to the highest place” is Philippians 2:9, “God has highly exalted him.”

“And gave to You the right to bear the name above all names” is that same verse. God “bestowed on him the name that is above every name.” The right to bear the name is the gift. “At the name of Jesus we should bow / Every tongue confess that You are Lord” is Philippians 2:10-11. “When You come in glory” is the return. “The risen King” assumes the resurrection and does not tell it. “Rival thrones will fall before the Lord of all” is the future reign, in the register of Philippians 2:10-11 and Revelation 19:16.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 4

Christ is the one who came, was pierced, was exalted, will be confessed, and will come again. The cross is explicit, and the iniquities are ours. The resurrection is the phrase “risen King,” not an account of the third day. Burial, wrath, and the empty tomb are unnamed. The finished work is in the song. It is not as fully told as Philippians 2 and 1 Corinthians 15 tell it. That is a 4.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 4

The verses declare what Jesus did and what God did. “We should bow” and “We will sing” are the church’s response, tied to his name and his coming. The chorus, “Hail to the King,” is praise of him. There is no private vow. “His splendor” in the chorus is about the King in the third person, after the verses have been speaking to him. The song stays on Christ.

---

## Description of God’s Character: 4

Jesus is God, man, the pierced one, the exalted Lord, King of kings, risen King, and the true and living God. Majesty and splendor are named. Holiness and mercy are not given their own lines. Justice is present as rival thrones falling before the Lord of all. The attributes that are here are the ones Philippians 2 and Isaiah 53 put on Christ.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 4

The chorus is short. The verses are where the church learns the incarnation, the cross, and the confession of lordship. Three times in two years of this window means the room may know the hail better than the verses. “In very nature God” and “iniquities” are ordinary Christian words and worth keeping. If the band teaches the chorus and skips verse 1, the room learns a coronation and not the cross.

---

## Tone

The tone is formal praise. The line that sets it is “Hail to the King / In all His splendor and majesty.” The verse under that hail is “Pierced for our iniquities as You hung upon the cross,” so the splendor is not detached from the cross when the verse is sung.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never says that he was buried, that he rose on the third day in so many words, that the death satisfied wrath, or that sinners must repent.

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** Philippians 2:5-11, Isaiah 53:5, or the return of Christ. Verse 1 belongs with the humiliation. Verse 2 belongs with the coming reign.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** A narrated resurrection, and repentance. Pair it with *Christ Our Hope in Life and Death* when the grave has to be answered in plain lines.
- **Diet sentence.** Three uses in two years add a Philippians 2 song, as long as verse 1 is sung with the chorus. It does not add the burial or a call to repent.
- **What not to do.** Do not drop verse 1 and live on “Hail to the King.” Sing “the right to bear the name” as the song has it. Philippians 2:9 in the reading says God bestowed that name.

## Why this overall label

Verse 1 sings the incarnation, the cross, and our iniquities, and the song goes on to the exaltation, the name God bestowed, the confession, and the coming reign. The resurrection is assumed in “risen King.” The church can use this regularly when the verses are actually sung.
