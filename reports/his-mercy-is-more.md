## Thesis

Our sins are many. God’s mercy is more. That mercy is the omniscient God refusing to count the sum against us, the Father calling wanderers home, and the blood-payment of a life we could not afford.

---

## Overall recommendation

**Excellent / Prefer**

Feature it. Sing all three verses. Verse 3 is the atonement stanza: “His blood was the payment, His life was the cost.” If you drop it, the congregation has praised mercy without naming the price.

The church has already sung it for at least four years (14 logs in the 2023–2026 snapshot). Keep the refrain intact. “Our sins they are many” is the confession the rest of the hymn answers.

## Voice and addressee

Corporate throughout: “our sins,” “we have done,” “we constantly roam,” “we stood ’neath a debt.” The refrain addresses the congregation with “Praise the Lord.” God is spoken about more than spoken to. There is no solo “I.”

---

## How the text moves

The refrain states the thesis first, then three verses explain it.

1. **Mercy and omniscience together.** He knows the sum and does not count it. Sins thrown into a sea without shore.
2. **Mercy as patience and welcome.** We roam. The Father calls home. He welcomes the weakest, the vilest, the poor.
3. **Mercy paid for.** Kindness lavished. Blood as payment. Life as cost. A debt we could never afford.

The hymn begins with the size of mercy and ends with the price of mercy. The refrain does not change, and it should not. The last line of every stanza is the same confession.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

**Do not drop verse 3.** Without blood and cost, “His mercy is more” can sound like leniency. Verse 3 is what keeps mercy from becoming mood.

**Do not drop “our sins they are many.”** That line is the honest half of the refrain. A band that sings only “praise the Lord, His mercy is more” has cut the confession.

No single line is false. The line that needs a careful ear is “What love could remember no wrongs we have done,” handled in the accuracy section.

## Persons of the Trinity named

The Father is named in verse 2 (“What Father so tender”). The Son is never named. His work is present as “His blood” and “His life.” The Spirit is unnamed. “Praise the Lord” is the covenant name in English dress, not a third distinct person in this text.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

Verse 3 stops a vague theist. “His blood was the payment, His life was the cost” is Christian atonement. Verses 1–2 plus the refrain are closer to what a moral monotheist could sing: sins many, mercy more, a tender Father calling home. Cutting the hymn after verse 2 makes the remaining text far less specifically Christian.

The name of Jesus can be absent and the third verse still will not fit another religion’s worship. Keep verse 3.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

**Unbiblical.** Nothing contradicts Scripture. “Omniscient, all-knowing, He counts not their sum” looks like a clash until “remember” is read as Scripture uses it. Hebrews 8:12 and Jeremiah 31:34: He remembers sins no more. That is refusal to count them against us, not a hole in His knowledge. The hymn says both halves on purpose.

**Non-biblical.** “Stronger than darkness” is a short image for mercy outrunning evil. It does not smuggle in a dualism.

**Thin.** Jesus is unnamed. Resurrection is unnamed. Wrath is unnamed. “New every morn” is Lamentations 3:22–23 in four words, which is enough, and still brief.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 5

**Refrain.** “Our sins they are many, His mercy is more” is Romans 5:20: where sin increased, grace abounded all the more. “New every morn” is Lamentations 3:22–23. “Stronger than darkness” belongs with John 1:5 and James 2:13 (mercy triumphs over judgment). The line does not treat darkness as a rival god.

**Verse 1.** “What love could remember no wrongs we have done / omniscient, all-knowing, He counts not their sum” holds 1 John 3:20 and Hebrews 8:12 together. God knows. God does not impute. “Thrown into a sea without bottom or shore” is Micah 7:19.

**Verse 2.** “What patience would wait as we constantly roam” is Romans 2:4 and Luke 15. “What Father so tender is calling us home” is Luke 15:20–24. “He welcomes the weakest, the vilest, the poor” is Luke 15:1–2, 1 Timothy 1:15, and James 2:5. The welcome is to sinners, not to the almost-respectable.

**Verse 3.** “What riches of kindness He lavished on us” is Ephesians 1:7–8 and 2:7. “His blood was the payment, His life was the cost” is 1 Peter 1:18–19, Mark 10:45, and 1 Corinthians 6:20. “We stood ’neath a debt we could never afford” is Colossians 2:14 and Romans 6:23. The people singing are debtors. Mercy is not a discount. It is a payment.

The lyric reads these texts in their ordinary sense. The only doctrinal load it asks the room to carry is the remember / omniscient pairing, and it carries both sides in the same couplet.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 4

The finished work is in verse 3: blood, life as cost, unpayable debt. Sin is named in every refrain. That is more gospel than a title list.

What keeps this category at 4 is the missing name of Jesus and the missing resurrection. The rubric warns against titles without the work. This hymn has the work without the name. A congregation that already knows whose blood it is will hear Jesus. Heard alone, verses 1–2 could be sung about the Father’s general kindness. Verse 3 is what ties kindness to atonement. Keep it.

Wrath, the cross as a word, and the empty tomb are also absent. The payment metaphor assumes them. It does not preach them.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 5

The hymn declares what God has done with our sin. The only “we” is confession and reception: we roam, we stood under a debt, we praise. There is no vow about the singer’s present intensity, and no romance. Affection is “praise the Lord,” after the mercy has been described.

Holiness and wrath are implied by debt and by “vilest,” and never named. The hymn is about mercy, which is why the category is a 4. Mercy here is still tied to payment.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 5

Plain words. A refrain a child can keep. The church already knows it (at least four years in the snapshot). Keys on file include D, Eb, and E.

Three verses will run long only if the band repeats the last refrain past the point of sense. If a cut is forced, do not cut verse 3, and do not cut “our sins they are many.”

The lyric already puts confession in the people’s mouth. You do not need a speech about meaning it.

---

## Tone

Glad and sober at once. The refrain is a shout. The verses keep naming sin, roaming, vileness, and debt. That mix is the right tone for mercy. Nothing cute, nothing sensual, nothing flippant.

The room is not asked to confer mercy on God. It is asked to praise the mercy He has already shown.

---

## What the congregation never says

They never say the name of Jesus.  
They never name the resurrection.  
They never name wrath or the word “cross.”  
They never name the Spirit.  
They never name lordship or repentance as words.  
They never name justification as a word (they do sing that He does not count the sum).

## Setlist notes

**When it serves the sermon.** Romans 5. Ephesians 2. Micah 7. Lamentations 3. Luke 15. Jeremiah 31 / Hebrews 8. A confession hymn before the sermon, or a response after sin has been exposed. Pairs cleanly with a song that names Jesus and the resurrection.

**Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** The name of Jesus. The empty tomb. Wrath when the text is Romans 3 or Isaiah 53. Lordship when the text is Luke 9 or Romans 6.

**What it adds to the year’s diet.** Repeated confession of “our sins,” mercy larger than that confession, and blood as payment. It will not supply resurrection, the name of Jesus, or a doctrine-of-God hymn beyond mercy and fatherhood.

**What not to do.** Do not program verses 1–2 and a long tag. Do not use the refrain under prayer while skipping the payment stanza.
