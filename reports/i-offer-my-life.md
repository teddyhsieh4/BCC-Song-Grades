## Thesis

The singer lays everything before the Lord (past and future, regrets and achievements, hopes and plans) and asks Him to use it for His glory as a pleasing sacrifice. The bridge gives the one reason in the lyric: the people can give God nothing He has not first given them. The song is a prayer of consecration. It never names Christ, sin, or the cross.

---

## Overall recommendation

Weak

Do not feature it. The church has logged it 7 times across at least four years of the 2023–2026 window, so the room knows it, but the lyric teaches very little. Every line is a true prayer for a Christian to pray, and the bridge rests on 1 Chronicles 29:14. The trouble is what the song leaves out. Romans 12:1 calls for this offering "by the mercies of God," and this lyric gives the offering with no mercies named: no Jesus, no cross, no forgiveness. If it is used at all, put it after the sermon on a Sunday when the gospel has already been sung plainly, keep the bridge, and keep it short.

---

## Alternative song suggestions

The lyric is reaching for consecration: handing over a whole life, including the past and the plans not yet made, to God's use. Three of these the church already sings. One is not in the catalog.

**Already sung**

- **Your Will Be Done** (Jonny Robinson / Rich Thompson, CityAlight, CCLI 7149566, graded Excellent / Prefer). The church gives its plans to the Father's will, and the song grounds that surrender in the Son who prayed the same prayer in Gethsemane and went to the cross.
- **All I Have Is Christ** (Jordan Kauflin, Sovereign Grace, CCLI 5174122, graded Excellent / Prefer). It tells the story of a sinner who was lost and rescued by Christ's death, and only then resolves to live for Him alone, confessing that even the strength to obey comes from Christ.
- **When I Survey the Wondrous Cross** (Isaac Watts / Lowell Mason, hymn, CCLI 27893, logged once, not yet graded). The last stanza asks the same thing this song asks, "my soul, my life, my all," and gives the reason: "love so amazing, so divine" seen at the cross.

**Not yet logged**

- **Take My Life and Let It Be** (Frances Ridley Havergal, 1874, hymn, public domain; no SongSelect number confirmed). Not in the catalog. It offers hands, feet, voice, money, will, and love one by one, and it asks God to do the consecrating ("Take myself, and I will be / Ever, only, all for Thee"). It covers the same ground in specific terms.

## Voice and addressee

The verses and chorus are first-person singular ("all that I am," "my heart and my hands"), spoken to an unnamed "Lord." The bridge shifts to "we" and asks a question of that same Lord. The whole song is prayer to God about what the singer is giving.

---

## How the text moves

The first verse hands over the past: what the singer is, has, regrets, and has achieved. The second verse hands over the future: hopes, plans, and dreams not yet fulfilled. The bridge then steps back and admits that all of it was God's gift in the first place. The chorus repeats the offering between each section and does not change.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

The bridge is the line to protect: "What can we give / That You have not given?" It is the only place the lyric says anything about God, and it keeps the offering from sounding like a gift the singer originates. Bands often skip a bridge for time. If this one goes, the song is the singer's vow and nothing else. No single line is false, so nothing here would move the label on its own.

## Persons of the Trinity named

No person of the Trinity is named. Every address is to "Lord." Context tells a Christian congregation who is meant, but the lyric itself never says Father, Son, Spirit, Jesus, or Christ.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

Yes, all of it. A devout Jew or Muslim, or anyone who believes in one God who gives life, could sing every verse, the chorus, and the bridge without changing a word. "Pleasing sacrifice" and "what do we have that is not already Yours" fit any faith that thinks of life as a gift to be returned to God. No line in the song would stop them.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** "wishes and dreams that are yet to come true" is mood language. It assumes the dreams will come true and does not say whether they are good ones.
- **Thin:** the chorus, which is most of the song. Offering one's days and praise to God is right, but the lyric gives no reason beyond the bridge's general "You have given," and it never says what God gave.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 3

Nothing in the lyric is false. "Lifting my praise to You / As a pleasing sacrifice" is close to Hebrews 13:15, and the whole offering of self is the language of Romans 12:1. The bridge is the strongest text in the song: "And what do we have / That is not already Yours?" is 1 Chronicles 29:14 and 1 Corinthians 4:7. "Everything I've been through / Use it for Your glory" is a fair prayer in light of Romans 8:28.

The score stays at 3 because each of those texts comes with a ground the song drops. Romans 12:1 rests on eleven chapters of God's mercy in Christ. Hebrews 13:15 says the sacrifice of praise is offered "through him," meaning Jesus. The lyric takes the response from these passages and leaves the reason behind.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 1

Christ does not appear in this song. His name is absent, no title points to Him, and nothing He did is mentioned. The rubric puts titles without the finished work at 2–3; this lyric has neither the titles nor the work. A prayer addressed to the Father can still rest on the Son's work. This lyric gives no sign that it does. In a church that wants the gospel in the people's mouths every week, that is the most serious gap a lyric can have.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 2

Almost every line reports what the singer is doing: "I lay them down," "I'm making them Yours," "Lord, I offer my life to You." These are large vows sung in the present tense, about everything the singer has and will have, and the song gives the congregation very little to stand on while they make them. The bridge is the one objective claim, that God already owns everything we could offer. It moves the score off 1, but it comes once, near the end.

---

## Description of God's Character: 2

The lyric tells us two things about God: He is Lord, and He is the giver of all we have. His glory is mentioned as the purpose of the offering. Holiness, justice, mercy, faithfulness, and sovereignty are not named. "Lord" is never filled in with anything specific.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 2

The words are simple and the room has sung it for years, so it goes easily. What the congregation learns by singing it is that a believer should give everything to God. That is true, and Scripture says it, but the lyric does not teach why, and the "I" voice in the verses makes it one person's vow repeated by many. The bridge is the most useful section because it is corporate and gives a reason. Ease and familiarity do not make up for how little doctrine the song carries.

---

## Tone

The tone is earnest and gentle. Nothing is flippant or sensual, and the intensity is moderate. The closest the lyric comes to overreach is "all that I am, all that I have / I lay them down," which asks every person in the room to promise everything, with no confession of how often we fail to keep that promise.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never names Jesus, sin, the cross, forgiveness, the resurrection, the Spirit's help to obey, or any mercy of God that would move a sinner to make this offering in the first place.

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** As a short response after a sermon on Romans 12:1–2, 1 Chronicles 29, stewardship, or a call to serve, and only when the message itself preached the mercies of God in Christ.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** Christ's death for sinners and the grace that makes an offering possible. Put a song that names the cross before it, such as *Jesus, Your Mercy* or *It Was Finished Upon That Cross*, so the people know why they are giving themselves away.
- **Diet sentence.** It adds a simple prayer of consecration that the room knows well. It adds nothing about Christ or the gospel, so it should be rare in a year's singing.
- **What not to do.** Do not drop the bridge. Do not use it to open the service, where nothing has yet been said about God, or as an altar-call song. Do not loop the last chorus line ("Lord, I offer You my life") as a tag. Repeating the vow does not supply the reason the song leaves out.

## Why this overall label

The lyric asks the congregation for everything and tells them almost nothing about the God who is receiving it. No line is false, and the bridge is good Scripture, but Christ is absent and the song can be sung word for word by someone who does not believe the gospel. It lasts in the set because it is easy, sincere, and broadly theistic, and the rubric holds songs like that at Weak.
