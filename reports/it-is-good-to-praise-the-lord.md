## Thesis

The lyric states that it is good to praise the Lord, to gaze upon His majesty, and to proclaim His love in the morning and His faithfulness at night. It then concludes that it is good to worship and praise the Lord. The text is a direct, short setting of Psalm 92:1–2.

---

## Overall recommendation

Acceptable with notes

Use sparingly. The lines are true and rest on a clear text. They teach almost nothing beyond the fact that praise is good and that God is loving and faithful. The song never names Christ, sin, the cross, or the resurrection. If it is used, put it after a sermon or another song that has already stated the gospel, and keep it short. The church has logged it six times across four years; the lyric supports only light use.

## Voice and addressee

The song is declarative. It addresses the Lord implicitly (“His majesty,” “His love”) and speaks of what it is good to do. No first-person vow dominates. The voice is corporate in function even if the pronouns are general.

---

## How the text moves

It states the goodness of praise, then adds the object of the gaze (majesty) and the content of the proclamation (love in the morning, faithfulness at night). It closes by restating that it is good to worship and praise the Lord. The movement is a single short claim repeated with modest expansion. There is no development beyond the psalm verse.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

The whole song is short. “It is good to praise the Lord” and “to proclaim His love in the morning / And His faithfulness at night” are the lines that carry the psalm. Drop either and the song loses the little content it has. No single line is false.

## Persons of the Trinity named

No person of the Trinity is named. The address is to the Lord. Context tells a Christian congregation who is meant.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

Yes. A devout Jew, Muslim, or any theist who believes in one God who is loving and faithful could sing every line without changing a word. No claim in the song would stop them.

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** none.
- **Thin:** the entire lyric. It restates Psalm 92:1–2 and adds “gaze upon His majesty.” It does not unfold the psalm or connect the praise to any work of God in Christ.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 3

The lines track Psalm 92:1–2 in ordinary language. “It is good to praise the Lord” and the proclamation of love and faithfulness day and night are accurate. The addition of “gaze upon His majesty” is consistent with biblical praise. The score stays at 3 because the lyric does nothing more than restate the verse. It does not teach the surrounding context of the psalm or connect the praise to any redemptive work.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 2

Christ is not named. No title points to Him. No work of the cross, substitution, or resurrection is mentioned. The rubric places titles without the finished work at 2–3; this lyric has neither the titles nor the work. A song of praise can still rest on the gospel. This one gives no sign that it does.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 4

The claims are about what is good to do and about the character of the Lord (majesty, love, faithfulness). The singer’s action is secondary. Desire does not dominate.

---

## Description of God’s Character: 3

God is the Lord whose majesty is gazed upon, whose love is proclaimed in the morning, and whose faithfulness is proclaimed at night. Those attributes are named. Holiness, justice, sovereignty, and mercy in Christ are not. The description is true but thin.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 3

The words are simple and the room has sung it for years, so it goes easily. What the congregation learns is that praise is good and that God is loving and faithful. That is true. It does not teach why the praise is due or what God has done to make it possible. Ease does not make up for the thinness.

---

## Tone

Gentle and affirmative. Nothing is flippant. The intensity is low.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never names Jesus, sin, the cross, forgiveness, the resurrection, or any mercy that would move a sinner to praise. It never says why the Lord is worthy of the praise the song declares is good.

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** A short response after a sermon on Psalm 92, praise, or the character of God, and only when the message or another song has already stated the gospel.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** Christ’s death for sinners and the grace that makes praise possible. Put a song that names the cross before or after it.
- **Diet sentence.** It adds a simple statement that praise is good and that God is loving and faithful. It adds nothing about Christ or the gospel, so it should be rare.
- **What not to do.** Do not use it to open a service where nothing has yet been said about God. Do not treat it as a full statement of why the Lord is to be praised.

## Why this overall label

The lyric is a true, short restatement of Psalm 92:1–2. Nothing is false. It never names Christ or the gospel, and a non-Christian could sing every line without tension. It is usable sparingly when the rest of the set supplies what it lacks. The thinness and the absence of the gospel keep it at Acceptable with notes rather than Strong.
