## Thesis

The believer has taken up his cross to follow Jesus and given up every other ambition, because God and heaven are still his. The world may despise him as it despised his Savior, and trouble may press on him, but trouble only drives him to Christ. He has called God "Abba, Father," and all things must work for his good. So the hymn tells his soul to remember its full salvation, the Spirit within, the Father's smile, and the Savior who died to win him, and to hurry on from grace to glory.

---

## Overall recommendation

Strong

Use it regularly, especially with sermons on discipleship and suffering. The church has logged it 6 times across at least four years of the 2023–2026 window. It deals honestly with the cost of following Christ and grounds that cost in grace. The singer can lose everything because "God and heav'n are still my own," and the fifth stanza names the Father, the Spirit, and the Savior who died. Its gospel is brief, and much of the hymn is the singer's own resolve. That keeps it from the Prefer lane.

## Voice and addressee

The first four stanzas are in the singular, addressed to Jesus ("Jesus, I my cross have taken, / All to leave and follow Thee"). In the fifth and sixth stanzas the singer turns and speaks to his own soul ("Think what Spirit dwells within thee," "Haste thee on from grace to glory"), as the psalmists do in Psalms 42 and 103. There is no "we."

---

## How the text moves

Stanza 1 is the vow to follow Christ and lose everything else. Stanzas 2 through 4 face what that costs: the world's contempt, loss of fame and treasure, and trouble of every kind. Each stanza answers the cost with God's favor. Stanza 5 turns from the cost to the singer's riches in the triune God. Stanza 6 looks ahead to glory, when faith becomes sight. The hymn moves from the cross the disciple carries to the heaven he will reach.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

Stanza 5 must stay: "Think what Spirit dwells within thee; / What a Father's smile is thine; / What a Savior died to win thee." It is the only place the hymn names the Spirit, the Father, and the Savior's death, and it is what keeps the first four stanzas from reading as the singer's own strength. "Yet how rich is my condition, / God and heav'n are still my own" in stanza 1 is the hymn's ground and should never be cut. No line is false.

The hymn assumes the gospel more than it tells it. Christ's death appears once, and the word "cross" in the title is the believer's cross from Matthew 16:24. That is a true use of the word. It does not tell the room what Christ's cross did.

---

## Persons of the Trinity named

All three are named in stanza 5: "Think what Spirit dwells within thee; / What a Father's smile is thine; / What a Savior died to win thee." The Father is also addressed in stanza 3 ("I have called Thee Abba, Father"), and Jesus is named in the first line.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

No. It begins with "Jesus," it calls God "Abba, Father," and it names the Spirit dwelling in the believer and the Savior who died to win him. Only a Christian could sing it. A cautious believer might pause at the size of the vows, which is the point of the hymn.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** "In Thy service pain is pleasure" is stated more strongly than Scripture puts it. James 1:2 and Romans 5:3 say to count trials as joy, but Scripture does not call pain pleasure. Read in context, the line means that loss for Christ is gain (Philippians 3:7–8).
- **Thin:** the gospel. The hymn's strength is discipleship and assurance. Christ's work is named in one line.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 5

The hymn follows the New Testament's teaching on discipleship and suffering closely. "Jesus, I my cross have taken, / All to leave and follow Thee" is Matthew 16:24 and Luke 14:33. "Let the world despise and leave me, / They have left my Savior too" is John 15:18–20. "Thou art not, like man, untrue" is Numbers 23:19 and 2 Timothy 2:13. "With Thy favor, loss is gain" is Philippians 3:7–8. "I have called Thee Abba, Father" is Romans 8:15, and "all must work for good to me" is Romans 8:28. "Man may trouble and distress me, / 'Twill but drive me to Thy breast" fits 2 Corinthians 4:8–10 and 12:9–10. "Think what Spirit dwells within thee" is Romans 8:9–11. "From grace to glory" and "Faith to sight" are 2 Corinthians 3:18 and 5:7.

The theology of the hymn is the theology of Romans 8 set as a disciple's prayer.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 3

Christ is the one the singer follows and the treasure he keeps when he loses all else. "Thou from hence my all shalt be" makes Christ supreme. But the gospel is told in one line, "What a Savior died to win thee," and the hymn says nothing of substitution, resurrection, or His reign. A believer singing it knows what that line means. The hymn itself does not explain it. That is a 3.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 3

The first four stanzas are the singer's vows and his response to trouble: "I my cross have taken," "Perish every fond ambition," "Go then, earthly fame and treasure!" These are large claims for a congregation to make about itself. They are tied to what God gives in return ("God and heav'n are still my own," "Show Thy face, and all is bright"), so they rest on grace. Stanzas 5 and 6 turn to objective truth about the Trinity and the hope of glory. The hymn is about half resolve and half grounds.

God is shown as a faithful Father who is wise, loving, and strong, and who governs every trouble for His child's good. Holiness and justice are not named.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 4

The hymn teaches the congregation what it costs to follow Christ, why that cost is worth it, and where its hope lies. The language is older ("repine," "fruition," "'twere not in joy to charm me"), and six long stanzas make it a long song, so a leader may need to cut some. The "I" voice fits a hymn of personal vows, though the room is making a large promise together. It is a 4 because the teaching is strong and the words take some work.

---

## Tone

The tone is steady and resolved. The first stanzas are sober about loss, and each one ends in confidence. The fifth stanza turns to encouragement, and the last stanza ends looking toward heaven.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never sings Christ's substitution, resurrection, or reign, never confesses sin as its own guilt, never names God's holiness or justice, and never sings in the plural voice.

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** After a sermon on Matthew 16:24–26, Luke 9:23 or 14:25–33, Philippians 3:7–14, Romans 8:14–39, 2 Corinthians 4, or 1 Peter 4:12–19. It fits a service on suffering, persecution, or the cost of discipleship.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** Christ's own cross. Put a song before it that tells what He did, such as *Hallelujah, What a Savior* or *Before the Throne of God Above*, so the disciple's cross is taken up in answer to His.
- **Diet sentence.** It adds the cost of discipleship and the assurance of adoption. It needs a gospel song beside it.
- **What not to do.** If stanzas must be cut, keep 1, 5, and 6, and choose one of 2 through 4. Do not cut stanza 5. Do not use it in place of a song about Christ's cross.

## Why this overall label

The hymn teaches the cost of following Christ, grounded in adoption, the Spirit's presence, and the hope of glory, and every line holds up against Scripture. Its gospel is one line, and its first half is the singer's vow. Paired with a song about Christ's cross, it is Strong.
