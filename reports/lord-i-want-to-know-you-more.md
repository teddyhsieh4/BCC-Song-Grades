## Thesis

When the singer is caught in self and in worldly wealth, the Spirit’s call melts those wanderings into love. The singer wants to know God more, to feel his heart and know his mind, and says that looking into his eyes stirs that cry. Daily faithfulness goes flat, and the arms of Jesus warm the hunger to be whole. The singer would give a final breath to know him in his death and resurrection.

---

## Overall recommendation

Acceptable with notes

Use it rarely, and do not drop the last line. The church has logged it 3 times across at least three years (2023, 2024, 2026). “I would give my final breath / To know You in Your death and resurrection” is the line that has to stay. “Looking in Your eyes” is the line to watch. It asks for a face-to-face feeling. The death and resurrection are Philippians 3:10 in one sentence, at the end. Sing that sentence, and let another song preach the cross if this one is used at all.

## Voice and addressee

First person singular, speaking to God. “I want to know You,” “feel Your heart,” “Looking in Your eyes.” Jesus is named in the third person in “the gentle arms of Jesus,” and then the prayer returns to “You” for “Your death and resurrection.” The Spirit is “His” call. The church would be singing one person’s hunger.

---

## How the text moves

It starts when the singer feels caught in self and bought by worldly wealth. Then a breeze blows, the singer knows the Spirit’s call, and the wanderings melt into love. The chorus asks to know God more, deep in the soul, to feel his heart and know his mind, and says that looking in his eyes stirs the cry. The second verse says daily deeds lose their song, sensitivity is gone, the race was run at the singer’s own pace, and the soul is shattered. The arms of Jesus warm the hunger to be whole. The closing line offers the singer’s final breath to know God in his death and resurrection. The chorus is desire. The last line is the only place the desire is tied to the cross and the grave.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

“I would give my final breath / To know You in Your death and resurrection” is the line the grade depends on. “The gentle arms of Jesus” names him before that line. “Looking in Your eyes stirs up within me / Cries that say I want to know You” is the line that will define the song if the ending is cut. The eyes are a feeling of nearness. The death and resurrection are the knowledge Philippians 3:10 actually names. No line is false. Dropping the final breath would leave a hunger to feel God’s heart and see his eyes.

## Persons of the Trinity named

Jesus is named, and his death and resurrection are the knowledge the singer asks for at the end. The Spirit is named as the one whose call comes like a breeze. The Father is unnamed. “You” in the chorus could be the Lord of the title. The last line makes the “You” the one who died and rose.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

The chorus can, if “You” is any god the singer wants to feel. “The gentle arms of Jesus” and “Your death and resurrection” cannot. A worshiper who does not confess Jesus has to stop there. “Looking in Your eyes” would still be singable about another beloved. That line is not the whole song once the last sentence is kept.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** “Looking in Your eyes stirs up within me.” The New Testament does not give the knowledge of Christ as a look into his eyes. “That’s when the breeze begins to blow / I know the Spirit’s call” makes the Spirit a feeling of wind. The call is real. The breeze is the song’s picture.
- **Thin:** “To feel Your heart and know Your mind” and “Deep within my soul I want to know You.” The desire to know God is right. Feeling the heart is vaguer than the death and resurrection the last line finally names.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 3

“I would give my final breath / To know You in Your death and resurrection” agrees with Philippians 3:10, “that I may know him and the power of his resurrection, and may share his sufferings, becoming like him in his death.” The song offers the last breath for that knowledge. It does not sing the fellowship of his sufferings in any further line.

“Just the time I feel that I’ve been caught / In the mire of self” and “bought by worldly wealth” name a real tangle. “The gentle arms of Jesus / Warm my hunger to be whole” says Jesus meets that hunger. “Looking in Your eyes” and “feel Your heart” are the chorus’s way of knowing him, and they are not Philippians 3:10. The last line is the text. The chorus is the desire in a softer shape. The score stays at 3 because the true ending is one sentence after a long feeling.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 3

Jesus is named. His death and resurrection are the knowledge the singer wants at the end. That is the finished work, in a single line, as the object of desire. The chorus never says the cross, the blood, or the grave. “Arms of Jesus” is nearness. The gospel is present as the thing to be known, and it is not told. The score is 3 because the death and resurrection are explicit and brief.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 2

The last line declares Christ’s death and resurrection as what the singer wants to know. The chorus is the singer’s interior: “I want to know You more,” “deep within my soul,” “feel Your heart,” “know Your mind,” “looking in Your eyes,” “cries that say I want to know You.” The verses are the singer’s dryness, self, wealth, and shattered soul. “I know the Spirit’s call” is a private recognition. The eyes and the hunger carry the song. The death and resurrection arrive as the last wish.

---

## Description of God’s Character: 3

The Spirit calls. Jesus has arms that warm a hungry soul, and he has a death and a resurrection the singer wants to know. Love is what the wanderings melt into. The heart and the mind are what the singer wants to feel and know. Holiness, justice, and wrath are unnamed. The death and resurrection imply the work of the Son. The chorus dwells on nearness and on eyes.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 3

Three logs across at least three years means it is occasional. The chorus is easy to memorize and easy to repeat without the ending. A congregation can learn a desire to know God, and, if the last line is sung, that the knowledge is of his death and resurrection. “Looking in Your eyes” will teach a softer knowledge if it is the line they take home. The words are ordinary. The last line is the teaching. It has to be sung, and it is the line a chorus-only use will skip.

---

## Tone

The tone is hungry and intimate. The line that sets it is “Looking in Your eyes stirs up within me / Cries that say I want to know You.” The verses are more honest about self and a shattered soul. The last line is graver than the eyes. The intimacy is the chorus. The death and resurrection are the close.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never says why Christ died, never says that sinners are forgiven by his blood, and never says that the Spirit’s call is the written gospel.

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** Philippians 3:10, if the final line is sung and not treated as optional. It does not serve a sermon on the atonement by itself.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** The reason for the death, and the forgiveness. Pair it with *In Christ Alone* (logged 8 times) or *Jesus, Your Mercy* (logged 14 times).
- **Diet sentence.** Three times in at least three years, it adds a desire to know Christ in his death and resurrection, after a long chorus about eyes and heart. It never says why he died.
- **What not to do.** Do not loop “looking in Your eyes.” Do not drop “death and resurrection.” Do not use the eyes as the picture of how this church knows the Lord.

## Why this overall label

The singer wants to know God, and the last line aims that knowledge at Christ’s death and resurrection. The chorus gets there by feeling his heart and looking into his eyes. The death and resurrection are real and brief. With the last line kept, and with the reason for the death sung somewhere else, the church can use this rarely.
