## Thesis

The singer asks that the words of the mouth and the meditations of the heart be pleasing to God, calls God rock and redeemer, and offers to be a blessing and a servant.

---

## Overall recommendation

Weak

Do not feature it. Four logs across 2023, 2024, and 2025 mean the room may already know the refrain. The opening prays Psalm 19:14. “Pleasing” is the NIV wording of that verse. The ESV says “acceptable.” The chorus leaves the law of the LORD, which is the body of the psalm, and adds “I desire to be a blessing” and “I want to be Your servant.” If it is used, put it after the whole psalm has been read, and keep the rock-and-redeemer line. Do not let the desire lines be the song.

---

## Alternative song suggestions

The lyric is reaching for words and a heart that please God, and for God as rock and redeemer, which is the close of Psalm 19. Two of these the church already sings. One is not in the catalog.

**Already sung**

**O Lord, My Rock and My Redeemer** (Nathan Stiff, Sovereign Grace, CCLI 7096627). Logged 8 times across at least four years (2023, 2024, 2025, 2026). It takes rock and redeemer and stays with the God who rescues his people.

**Speak, O Lord** (Keith Getty / Stuart Townend, Getty, CCLI 4615235). Logged 7 times across at least four years (2023, 2024, 2025, 2026). The church asks the Lord to shape them by his word, which is the part of Psalm 19 this chorus does not sing.

**Not yet logged**

**The Heavens Declare Thy Glory, Lord** (Isaac Watts, hymn on Psalm 19, public domain). Not in the catalog. No SongSelect number was confirmed in this check. It sings the heavens and the perfect law, the parts of Psalm 19 before verse 14.

## Voice and addressee

First person singular, addressed to God (“You,” “my God,” “Lord”). The church sings one person’s prayer. “You’re my Rock and my Redeemer” is said to him.

---

## How the text moves

It opens by asking that words and meditations be pleasing, and it repeats that ask. It then names God as rock and redeemer and the reason for the song, and it offers a desire to be a blessing and a servant in every hour. The end returns to the opening prayer. The chorus does not change the psalm line into a story of redemption. It adds the singer’s desire.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

“May the words of my mouth / And the meditations of my heart / Be pleasing to You, my God” is the line from the psalm. “You’re my Rock and my Redeemer” is the other half of Psalm 19:14 and should stay with it. “I desire to be a blessing in Your eyes” and “I want to be Your servant” are the lines a band can drop without losing the verse. No line is false. Dropping the psalm lines and keeping the desire would leave a thinner song than this already is. The psalm lines have to stay.

## Persons of the Trinity named

The addressee is “my God,” “Lord,” “Rock,” and “Redeemer.” Father, Son, and Spirit are unnamed. “Redeemer” can be said of the LORD in Psalm 19:14 and of Christ. The song does not say which.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

Much of it, yes. A worshiper who prays to God and wants a pleasing life can sing the opening and “I want to be Your servant.” “You’re my Rock and my Redeemer” is harder for someone who will not confess a redeemer, and it is still a title with no story of the redemption. If that line is dropped, the rest of the song works as a general prayer to be acceptable and useful.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** none.
- **Thin:** “I desire to be a blessing in Your eyes” and “Every hour, every moment / Lord, I want to be Your servant.” Service is right. The lines do not say what the servant does, or how a sinner becomes pleasing. “You’re the reason that I sing” states a motive and stops.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 3

Psalm 19:14 in the ESV reads, “Let the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be acceptable in your sight, O LORD, my rock and my redeemer.” The song says “meditations,” plural. “Pleasing to You” is the NIV wording of the same verse. “You’re my Rock and my Redeemer” keeps the verse’s own names. The rest of Psalm 19 is not in the song. The heavens declare the glory of God. The law of the LORD is perfect and revives the soul. The psalmist asks to be kept back from presumptuous sins. Verse 14 is the prayer after that. Sung alone, it is the closing sentence without the law, the warning, or the sins.

The chorus is the singer’s addition. “I desire to be a blessing” and “I want to be Your servant” are not Psalm 19. They are a sincere offer that Psalm 19 does not make.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 2

“Redeemer” is the nearest gospel word, and it is a title. The song never says that anyone was redeemed, or by what blood, or from what. There is no cross and no resurrection. The category stays at 2.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 2

The opening is a prayer, which a psalm may be. The chorus turns to the singer’s desire. “I desire to be a blessing in Your eyes.” “Every hour, every moment / Lord, I want to be Your servant.” “You’re the reason that I sing” is the singer’s own reason for singing. “You’re my Rock and my Redeemer” is the one line in the chorus that names God by what the psalm calls him. The desire lines are the ones that will be remembered with the refrain. They are about the singer’s offering.

---

## Description of God’s Character: 3

God is rock and redeemer, and he is the one whose sight the singer wants to please. Those two names are Psalm 19:14. Holiness, justice, mercy, and wrath are not added. The law of the LORD, which Psalm 19 calls perfect, sure, right, pure, clean, and true, is outside the lyric. Rock and redeemer are specific. They stand alone.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 3

Four times across three years means the refrain may already be familiar. The words are easy. What the church learns is the closing prayer of Psalm 19 and a wish to be a pleasing servant. It does not learn the glory of God in the heavens or the law that revives the soul. The singular “my mouth” and “I want” makes a private prayer into the congregation’s song, which is fair when the psalm has just been read and thin when it has not. The C variant in the catalog is a key label.

---

## Tone

The tone is quiet and earnest. The line that sets it is “Be pleasing to You, pleasing to You.” The servant lines keep that same soft offer. Nothing in the lyric is harsh, and nothing in it is playful.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never says that the law of the LORD is perfect, never names sin or presumptuous sins, and never says how God redeems.

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** Psalm 19, after the whole psalm has been read, especially verse 14 as the prayer that closes it. A sermon on speech and the heart can use the opening if the law and the redeemer have already been in the reading.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** The law of the LORD, and the cross. Pair it with *Speak, O Lord* when the service still needs the word that shapes the mouth, and with *O Lord, My Rock and My Redeemer* when the title needs the rescue.
- **Diet sentence.** Four uses in three years add a familiar prayer from the end of Psalm 19. It never adds the law, the heavens, or the blood.
- **What not to do.** Do not loop “I desire to be a blessing” and skip “my rock and my redeemer.” Do not use this as the gospel song.
