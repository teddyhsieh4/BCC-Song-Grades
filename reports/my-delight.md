## Thesis

The Father is the singer’s portion, hope, and delight. Jesus is the singer’s treasure, pure and kind. The Spirit is the singer’s comfort and the presence the singer needs day and night. Each stanza ends with “I love You.”

---

## Overall recommendation

Weak

Do not feature it. It appears once in this window, in 2026. Father, Jesus, and Spirit are each named, which is the song’s one clear gift. The Son is “so pure and so kind,” with no death and no resurrection. The refrain is “I love You, my delight.” “I need Your presence day and night” asks for the Spirit as nearness. If it is used, sing all three stanzas, and let another song preach the cross. The names alone are not a reason to feature it.

---

## Alternative song suggestions

The lyric is reaching for delight in the Triune God, and for Jesus as the treasure. Two of these the church already sings. One is not in the catalog.

**Already sung**

**All I Have Is Christ** (Jordan Kauflin, Sovereign Grace, CCLI 5174122). Logged 11 times across at least four years (2023, 2024, 2025, 2026). Christ is the treasure, and the lyric says why: he suffered in the singer’s place.

**I Will Glory in My Redeemer** (Steve Cook / Vikki Cook, Sovereign Grace, CCLI 3337435). Logged 9 times across at least four years (2023, 2024, 2025, 2026). The glory is in the Redeemer whose blood ransomed the singer.

**Not yet logged**

**Come, Thou Almighty King** (anonymous, about 1757, hymn, public domain). Not in the catalog. No SongSelect number was confirmed in this check. It addresses Father, Son, and Spirit, and asks the Spirit to rule in the people.

## Voice and addressee

First person singular, speaking in turn to the Father, to Jesus, and to the Spirit (“You”). Each stanza ends “Lord, I love You, my delight.” The delight is the singer’s love, offered to each person.

---

## How the text moves

The Father is portion, hope, and delight. Jesus is treasure, pure and kind. The Spirit is comfort and a presence needed day and night. The refrain does not change. Nothing after the Spirit stanza tells a saving act. The song’s movement is the three names.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

All three stanzas have to stay if the song is sung, because the point of the text is that the same love is offered to Father, Son, and Spirit. Dropping the Jesus stanza would hide the thin line, “so pure and so kind,” and would also hide that the Son was included. Dropping the Spirit stanza would leave a song about Father and Son only. No line is false. Cutting “so pure and so kind” would not give the Son a cross. No single line would change the label on its own.

## Persons of the Trinity named

The Father, Jesus, and the Spirit are each named and each given a stanza. That is the song’s real doctrinal act. The three are not confused with one another. They are also not given the relations Scripture gives them: the Son is not said to be sent, and the Spirit is not said to glorify the Son.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

Not the whole song. “Jesus, You are my treasure” and “Spirit, You are my comfort” name the Son and the Spirit. The Father stanza alone, “You are my portion in this life / And You are my hope and my delight,” could be sung by a worshiper who never confesses the Son. The refrain “I love You, my delight” does not say why.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** none.
- **Thin:** “You are so pure and so kind.” Purity and kindness are true of Jesus and are said with no text and no act. “I need Your presence day and night” asks for nearness. John 14:16 promises “another Helper, to be with you forever.” The song says “presence.” John 14:16 names “another Helper.” “I love You” is repeated in place of a reason.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 3

“Father, You are my portion in this life” stands near Psalm 73:26, “God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever.” The psalm says “forever.” The song says “in this life” and adds hope and delight. “Jesus, You are my treasure” is a true honor and not a verse. “Pure and kind” does not quote a text. “Spirit, You are my comfort” is near the promise of the Helper in John 14:16, and near the God of comfort in 2 Corinthians 1:3-4. The song’s next line, “I need Your presence day and night,” turns the person of the Spirit into the experience of presence. The names of the three persons are accurate. The sentences attached to Jesus and the Spirit are light.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 2

Jesus is named as treasure, pure and kind. That is the whole Christology of the song. There is no incarnation, no cross, no resurrection, and no priestly work. The Father’s stanza and the Spirit’s stanza do not supply it. A treasure who is pure and kind, with no death and no resurrection, stays at 2.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 2

Each stanza ends in the same confession of love. “And I love You, yes I love You / Lord, I love You, my delight.” The opening titles, portion and treasure and comfort, are about God. They arrive as “my portion,” “my treasure,” and “my comfort,” and the refrain is the singer’s love. There is no vow of obedience. The repeated line is desire and affection. That affection is most of what the church will sing.

---

## Description of God’s Character: 3

The Father is portion, hope, and delight. Jesus is treasure, pure, and kind. The Spirit is comfort and presence. Three persons are distinguished, which is more than a general “God is love.” Holiness is the word “pure,” said once, of Jesus. Justice, wrath, mercy, and truth are unnamed. Kindness is named. The cross that shows the kindness is not.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 3

The form is easy: three short stanzas and one refrain. One use, in 2026, means it is not yet a song the room knows. What the church can learn is the names of the Father, the Son, and the Spirit, and a few words for each. What it cannot learn is why Jesus is the treasure or what the Spirit was sent to do. The singular “I love You” is simple enough to sing together and thin as the only confession. All three stanzas are the useful part. The refrain alone is not.

---

## Tone

The tone is warm and personal. The line that sets it is “Lord, I love You, my delight.” “So pure and so kind” matches that warmth. The affection is the texture of every stanza.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never names sin, the cross, the resurrection, the sending of the Son, or the Spirit’s work of glorifying Christ.

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** A sermon that has already distinguished the three persons, if a short sung response is wanted. Psalm 73:26 can use the Father stanza after “portion forever” has been read.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** The cross and the resurrection. Pair it with *All I Have Is Christ* or *I Will Glory in My Redeemer*.
- **Diet sentence.** Once in 2026, it can add an explicit naming of Father, Son, and Spirit in one short song. It never adds the death or the resurrection.
- **What not to do.** Do not sing only the refrain. Do not drop the Jesus stanza and then call the song Trinitarian. Do not treat “presence day and night” as a theology of the Spirit.

## Why this overall label

The song speaks to the Father, to Jesus, and to the Spirit by name. The words for the Son are purity and kindness, and the words for the Spirit are comfort and presence. The line the church repeats is “I love You.” That is too thin to feature.
