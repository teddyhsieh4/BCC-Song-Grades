## Thesis

The singer's life is an altar on which he offers his soul, mind, and strength, cleansed by God's mercy, to live worthy of the One who called him. He loves Jesus because Jesus authored his rescue and raised him from death to life, and the Spirit in him shows him God's glory. So he asks Jesus to be glorified and magnified in his life, and chooses to lose his life and find it in Christ.

---

## Overall recommendation

Acceptable with notes

Use it now and then, as a response song after the gospel has been preached. The church has logged it 6 times across at least four years of the 2023–2026 window. The song comes from a Sovereign Grace album on Romans and is a setting of Romans 12:1. Its offering rests on God's mercy and Christ's rescue, which is the order Paul gives. It is short, though, and almost every line is about the singer's offering. It names Christ's rescue without saying what the rescue was. It is useful after a sermon that has already done that work.

## Voice and addressee

The singer speaks as "I" throughout and addresses Jesus as "You" ("How could I not love You? / You authored my rescue"). The chorus is a prayer that Jesus be "glorified" and "magnified." There is no "we."

---

## How the text moves

The first verse states the offering and its ground: a life on the altar, cleansed by mercy, worthy of the One who called. The chorus asks that Jesus be glorified in it. The second verse gives the reason for love: Christ's rescue, new life, and the Spirit. The bridge is a short vow to lose life and find it in Him. The song moves from mercy received to life given.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

"Cleansed by Your mercy" in the first verse and "You authored my rescue / Raised me up from death to life" in the second verse are what make the offering a response to grace. Without them the song is a vow of devotion and nothing more. Both verses should be sung. No line is false.

## Persons of the Trinity named

Jesus is named in the chorus. The Spirit is named in the second verse ("Your Spirit is in me, revealing Your glory"). The Father is not named. "The One who called my name" could mean the Father, who calls in Romans 8:30. The song addresses Jesus throughout, so it reads most naturally as Jesus.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

No. The chorus is addressed to Jesus, and the second verse names His rescue and the Spirit dwelling in the singer. Only a Christian could sing it. Still, the claims about Christ are brief, and a nominal believer could sing it as a pledge of devotion without knowing the gospel behind it.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** none. "Your Spirit is in me, revealing Your glory" fits 2 Corinthians 3:18 and 4:6.
- **Thin:** the gospel. "You authored my rescue" names salvation and leaves it there. The chorus repeats a short prayer.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 4

The song is close to its source text. "This life is an altar where I want to offer" and "Cleansed by Your mercy" are Romans 12:1, presenting the body as a living sacrifice "by the mercies of God." "My soul, and my mind, and strength" is Mark 12:30. "To live a life worthy / Of the One who called my name" is Ephesians 4:1. "Raised me up from death to life" is Ephesians 2:4–5. "I choose to lose my life, Lord / And find it in You" is Matthew 16:25. "Jesus, be glorified" fits Philippians 1:20, Christ honored in my body.

It is a 4 because the song quotes the response half of Romans 12:1 and gives the mercies only a line or two. The doctrine is right and short.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 3

Jesus is the one the singer loves and wants glorified, and He is credited with the singer's rescue and new life. That is more than a title. But the cross, His death, and His resurrection are not named, and the song never says what the rescue was. The rubric puts a song that names Christ's saving work without the cross at 3.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 3

The song is about the singer's offering of himself. "Where I want to offer," "Oh what joy as I give my life," and "I choose to lose my life" are the singer's acts and feelings. They are grounded in God's mercy and Christ's rescue, so the song keeps the right order of grace and response. Most of its lines are still about the singer, and the bridge is a vow.

---

## Description of God's Character: 3

| Attribute | Line |
|---|---|
| Mercy | "Cleansed by Your mercy" |
| Saving power | "You authored my rescue," "Raised me up from death to life" |
| Calling | "the One who called my name" |
| Glory | "revealing Your glory," "be glorified" |

The attributes are tied to salvation and are true. Holiness, justice, sovereignty, and faithfulness are not named.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 3

A congregation can learn it in one hearing. It teaches the congregation the shape of Romans 12:1: God's mercy first, then a life given back. That is a useful lesson after a sermon on Romans 12 or a call to discipleship. But the song is in the singular, the gospel content is two lines, and the chorus and bridge repeat short phrases. The room learns one point.

---

## Tone

The tone is quiet and devoted. The second verse lifts with "Oh what joy as I give my life." Nothing is sentimental or romantic. The singer's love is tied to what Jesus did.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never names the cross, Christ's death, His resurrection, or sin, never names the Father, never sings God's holiness or justice, and never sings as "we."

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** After a sermon on Romans 12:1–2, Ephesians 4:1, Matthew 16:24–26, or 2 Corinthians 5:14–15. It fits as the last song, when the congregation responds to what has been preached.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** The cross and resurrection. Sing a song that tells Christ's work, such as *How Great You Are* or *Hallelujah, What a Savior*, earlier in the set so that "You authored my rescue" has content.
- **Diet sentence.** It adds a short response song grounded in mercy. It adds no account of the gospel, so it should follow a song that gives one.
- **What not to do.** Do not open a service with it before mercy has been sung or preached. Do not drop the second verse, which carries the rescue and the Spirit.

## Why this overall label

The song sets Romans 12:1 in the right order, with mercy first and a life given in response, and it names Jesus, His rescue, and the Spirit. It never names the cross or the resurrection, and most of its lines are about the singer's offering. It is usable after a set or sermon that has preached the cross.
