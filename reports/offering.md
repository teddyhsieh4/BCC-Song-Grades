## Thesis

God is so holy and so bright that no one could stand before His throne, and the singer comes only because of Jesus' blood and mercy. Having come, the singer brings worship to Jesus as King, the only one on earth who deserves it. The song's doctrines are God's holiness and glory, access to God through Christ's blood, and the worthiness of Jesus to receive honor.

---

## Overall recommendation

Acceptable with notes

Use it now and then, as a short response of worship once the set or the sermon has already preached the cross. The church has logged it 7 times across at least four years of the 2023–2026 window. The verse is the reason to sing it: "It's only by Your blood" gives a real ground for coming near a holy God, and that line puts it above a generic praise chorus. The song is one verse and a repeated chorus, though, and that one blood line is the whole of its gospel. It never names sin, the cross, or the resurrection, so it cannot be the place in the set where the gospel is told.

## Voice and addressee

The singer speaks as "I" from start to finish ("Lord, I come," "the praises that I sing"). Every line is addressed to God. The verse speaks to the "Holy One of heaven" on His throne, and the chorus names that same addressee as "Jesus" and "my King." The Christmas version the band uses at Christmas switches part of the chorus to "we" (see Identification notes).

---

## How the text moves

The verse starts at God's throne with His glory and holiness, then turns to the singer's only way in, which is His blood and mercy. The chorus answers by bringing worship and asking that Jesus receive the honor He is owed. The chorus repeats and does not add a new claim, so the song's argument is finished by the end of the first verse.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

The verse has to stay, and above all the two lines "It's only by Your blood" and "only through Your mercy, Lord, I come." Without them the song is a chorus of a person bringing praise to a King, and nothing says why a mortal may approach a throne no one "would dare" to stand before. Some bands start on the chorus or repeat it at the end without going back to the verse. If the verse is sung once at the top and then the chorus loops five times, most of the singing is about the singer's offering and very little is about the blood. No single line is false, so no line on its own would change the label.

The barrier the verse names is that the singer is "mortal." Scripture's barrier is that we are sinners before a holy God (Psalm 24:3–4, Isaiah 6:5). The word "Holy" in the next line points toward that, but the lyric never says sin. "Your blood" is the closest the song comes to the cross. It tells the room what gives them access. It does not say that Christ died in their place or bore anything for them, so substitution is implied rather than stated.

---

## Persons of the Trinity named

The Son is named: "Jesus" in the chorus, and "Your blood" in the verse points to Him. The verse's "Holy One of heaven" on the throne reads most naturally as the same Jesus, since the chorus addresses Him in the same breath. The Father is not separately named, and the Spirit is not named.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

A vague theist could sing the first half of the verse, about light, glory, and a throne no mortal would approach, and the chorus could be sung to any king if the one word "Jesus" were dropped. The line that stops them is "It's only by Your blood." No one can sing that honestly without believing that a death made the way to God. A Muslim or Jew would stop there, and again at "Jesus, may You receive the honor," which gives Jesus the worship due to God.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** "The sun cannot compare / To the glory of Your love" is a poetic picture. It is close to Revelation 21:23, where the glory of God gives the city its light, but the lyric makes the comparison about His love, which no text says in those terms.
- **Thin:** the chorus. "I bring an offering of worship to my King" is right, but it says only that the singer is worshiping. With the verse gone or sung once, the repeated chorus gives the congregation little to learn.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 4

The verse can be tied to texts line by line. "There is no shadow in Your presence" is 1 John 1:5, "God is light, and in him is no darkness at all." "No mortal man would dare / To stand before Your throne / Before the Holy One of heaven" is the question of Psalm 24:3 and Psalm 130:3, and the fear of Isaiah 6:5. "It's only by Your blood ... Lord, I come" is Hebrews 10:19–22: we have confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus. The chorus line "No one on earth deserves the praises that I sing" is Revelation 5:12, where the Lamb who was slain is worthy to receive honor and glory, and "the honor that You're due" echoes Psalm 29:2.

The score is 4 and not 5 because the song is short and the verse states each truth once without unfolding it. The weakest spot is "mortal." It names the creature's limit and leaves out the sinner's guilt, which is why Psalm 24 says only clean hands may ascend. Nothing in the lyric contradicts Scripture.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 3

Christ is named and given the worship due to God, and His blood is the singer's only way into the presence of the holy God. That is more than titles alone, and it is the line that lifts the song above a 2. But the gospel in this lyric is one line long. There is no cross, no death named as a death, no sin borne, and no resurrection. "Your blood" tells the people that Christ's sacrifice opens the way, and the song moves straight to the singer's offering of praise without saying what that sacrifice accomplished. That fits a 3.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 3

The verse is objective. It describes God's glory, His holiness, and the way of access, and the singer is only the one who comes. The chorus is about what the singer is doing: "I bring an offering of worship." That act is not a feeling, and the chorus keeps its eye on Jesus ("may You receive the honor"), so the song does not slide into emotional report. There is no large vow. Still, the repeated chorus means the song spends most of its time on the singer's offering, and the objective content is all in one verse sung once or twice.

---

## Description of God's Character: 3

The lyric names these attributes:

| Attribute | Line |
|---|---|
| Glory | "the glory of Your love" |
| Light, no darkness in Him | "There is no shadow in Your presence" |
| Holiness | "the Holy One of heaven" |
| Kingship, sovereignty | "Your throne," "my King" |
| Mercy | "only through Your mercy" |
| Worthiness | "No one on earth deserves the praises" |

Holiness and mercy are held together in the verse, which is the song's strength: the throne is unapproachable, and mercy makes the way. Justice, wrath, faithfulness, and God's work in history are not in the lyric. Each attribute is a single phrase.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 3

The room knows the song, and the melody is easy to sing. What the congregation learns by singing it is that a holy God is approached only by the blood and mercy of Jesus, and that He alone deserves their praise. That is a true and useful lesson. It is one lesson, and it comes once. The "I" voice is fine for a song of personal approach, though it gives the people less to confess together than a "we" text would. The chorus will be sung four or five times for each pass through the verse, so the teaching is a small share of the minutes.

---

## Tone

The tone is reverent and quiet. The fear in "No mortal man would dare" sets the mood, and the chorus answers it with humble worship. The singer asks that Jesus "receive the honor that You're due," which gives Him what is already His by right.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never names its own sin, never sings the cross or Christ's death in their place, never mentions the resurrection, repentance, the Father's sending, the Spirit, or justification. Outside the Christmas version, it never moves from "I" to "we."

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** After a sermon on Hebrews 10:19–22, Hebrews 4:14–16, Isaiah 6, Psalm 24, or Revelation 4–5, where approaching a holy God by the blood of Christ is the point. It also works after the Lord's Supper, when the blood has just been named at the table. At Christmas, the Christmas version fits a sermon on Matthew 2 or Luke 2, since its added verse tells of the wise men and shepherds coming to worship.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** Sin, the cross, and the resurrection. Put a song that tells the gospel story before it, such as *It Was Finished Upon That Cross* or *Before the Throne of God Above*, so the people know what "Your blood" means when they sing it.
- **Diet sentence.** It adds a short song of approach and worship that names the blood as the way in. It never adds the story of the cross or the empty tomb, so it should not be a weekly fixture.
- **What not to do.** Do not drop the verse or sing the chorus only. Do not end on a long loop of "I bring an offering to You," which repeats the singer's act and leaves the blood line far behind. Do not open the service with it before anything has been said about the gospel.

## Identification notes

This report grades the standard text: one verse ("The sun cannot compare"), a pre-chorus ("It's only by Your blood"), and the chorus. At Christmas the band uses the Christmas version of the same song, which accounts for three of the setlist variants. That version adds a first verse about the star over Bethlehem, the shepherds, and the wise men bowing before "the humble Prince of Peace," and a later chorus in the "we" voice. The added verse supplies the incarnation the standard text lacks. It does not add the cross or the resurrection, so the grade is the same for both versions.

PraiseCharts files the standard and Christmas versions under the same CCLI 3956562, with the copyright line 2003 Integrity's Hosanna! Music. That is the number on the catalog row. The older row listed CCLI 4216091 and year 2002. Integrity also lists a separate "Christmas Offering" (2013, CCLI 7005509), recorded by Casting Crowns. The band does not use that listing.

---

## Why this overall label

The verse gives the congregation a real reason to come before a holy God: the blood and mercy of Jesus. That one line, with the reverent picture of the throne, makes the song usable. The rest is a short chorus of worship, and the lyric never tells the story behind the blood, so it needs a set that has already sung the cross.
