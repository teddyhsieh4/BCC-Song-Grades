## Thesis

The blood of Jesus is the only cleansing power. It washes guilt and shame. His name is the name by which we are saved, because he bore the cross the singer should have borne, a ransom for the singer’s sin.

---

## Overall recommendation

Strong

Use it at the Lord’s table or with a sermon on the blood, and sing both stanzas. Two logs, in 2025 and 2026, is the record in this window. “He bore the cross I should have had / A ransom for my sin” is the line that has to stay. “I’m forever grateful / and I will always trust” is the singer’s response, and it is safe only because the blood and the ransom are in the same song. The resurrection is not here. Pair a song that says he rose.

## Voice and addressee

The first stanza mixes testimony and a vow. “My guilt and shame are washed away” and “I’m forever grateful / and I will always trust” are the singer. The second stanza says “we’re saved” and then returns to “the cross I should have had” and “my sin.” Jesus is spoken about in the third person. The church is confessing the blood and then promising trust.

---

## How the text moves

It starts with the blood as the only cleansing power and with guilt and shame washed away. The singer answers with gratitude and trust, and names that blood “the sacrifice of love.” The second stanza moves from the blood to the name, and then says why the name saves: he bore the singer’s cross, a ransom for the singer’s sin. The claim gets more specific in the second stanza. It does not go on to the resurrection.

---

## Lines you cannot cut

“He bore the cross I should have had / A ransom for my sin” is the line the grade depends on. “The precious blood of Jesus / The only cleansing power” and “My guilt and shame are washed away” are the other lines that have to stay with it. “I’m forever grateful / and I will always trust” is the response. Sung without the blood and the ransom, that response is a personal promise with nothing under it. No line is false. Dropping the ransom line would leave cleansing and gratitude and would weaken the substitution. The ransom line has to stay.

## Persons of the Trinity named

Jesus is named, and his blood and his name are the subject. The Father and the Spirit are unnamed. “The name by which we’re saved” does not go on to say that the name is the name of the Son given by the Father.

---

## Could another worshiper sing this unchanged?

No. “The precious blood of Jesus,” “He bore the cross I should have had,” and “a ransom for my sin” cannot be moved to another savior. Someone who admires sacrifice in general cannot sing “the cross I should have had” without accepting that the cross was his own due. The gratitude lines, alone, could be sung about almost any gift. They are not the song.

---

## Unbiblical, non-biblical, thin

- **Unbiblical:** none.
- **Non-biblical:** none.
- **Thin:** “Beneath its crimson flood.” The blood cleanses. “Flood” is the song’s heightening of that blood, in the manner of fountain hymns, and it is not a verse. “I’m forever grateful” states the feeling and relies on the lines around it.

---

## Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity: 4

“The precious blood of Jesus / The only cleansing power” agrees with 1 John 1:7, “the blood of Jesus his Son cleanses us from all sin,” and with 1 Peter 1:18-19, redemption “with the precious blood of Christ.” “My guilt and shame are washed away” is what that cleansing means for guilt. “The only cleansing power” is a strong claim. The New Testament puts that cleansing in his blood and does not offer a second one.

“The precious name of Jesus / The name by which we’re saved” agrees with Acts 4:12, “there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved.” “He bore the cross I should have had / A ransom for my sin” says substitution. Mark 10:45 says the Son of Man came “to give his life as a ransom for many.” The song says “for my sin” and “the cross I should have had.” That is the right exchange. It does not say “for many,” and it does not say he rose. “Crimson flood” is poetic volume added to the blood. The ransom line is accurate. The song stops before the resurrection.

---

## Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated: 4

Jesus is present as the one whose blood cleanses and whose name saves, and as the one who bore the singer’s cross. Guilt, shame, and sin are named. The cross and the ransom are explicit. Burial and resurrection are not. Wrath is not. A ransom for sin on the cross the singer should have borne is the finished work in short lines, without the empty tomb. That is a 4.

---

## Objectivity vs. Subjectivity: 3

The blood, the guilt, the name, the cross, and the ransom are declarations. “I’m forever grateful / and I will always trust” is the singer’s vow. “I will always trust” is a large promise. It comes after the blood has been named, which is the right ground for trust, and it is still the singer’s claim about his future. The second stanza returns to “I” in “the cross I should have had” and “my sin,” which is testimony of substitution. The vow keeps the score at 3.

---

## Description of God’s Character: 3

The song’s subject is the blood and the name. Love is in the title, “the sacrifice of love,” and it is love as the cross borne for sin. Cleansing power is what the blood does. Holiness, justice, and wrath are unnamed, though a ransom for sin assumes a real debt. Mercy is in the washing of guilt. The character of God is shown in that one act. It is not described beyond it.

---

## Congregational Usefulness and Clarity: 4

Two uses, in 2025 and 2026, means it is known to some and not a staple. The lines are short and the exchange is clear: his cross, my sin. A congregation can learn that the blood of Jesus cleanses guilt and that he bore the cross they should have borne. “Crimson flood” may need no explanation once “washed away” has been sung. The vow “I will always trust” is singable and should not be the line the band repeats by itself. The second stanza is the one that teaches the ransom. Both stanzas belong at the table.

---

## Tone

The tone is grateful and direct. The line that sets it is “I’m forever grateful / and I will always trust,” after “My guilt and shame are washed away.” The ransom line is plainer and heavier than “crimson flood.” The song does not play with the blood. It names it as the cleansing and as the price.

---

## What the congregation never says

The congregation never says that he rose, that he reigns, that wrath was borne, or that the Spirit applies the blood.

---

## Setlist notes

- **When it serves the sermon.** 1 Peter 1:18-19, 1 John 1:7, Mark 10:45, Acts 4:12, or the Lord’s table. The second stanza belongs with the ransom. The first belongs with the blood and the guilt.
- **Gaps the rest of the hour must cover.** The resurrection. Pair it with *Christ Our Hope in Life and Death* or *Yet Not I but Through Christ in Me* so the ransom is not left without the grave overcome.
- **Diet sentence.** Twice in two years, it adds a short, explicit ransom and a cleansing of guilt. It never adds the resurrection.
- **What not to do.** Do not loop “I’m forever grateful” and skip the ransom. Do not drop “I should have had.” Do not treat “crimson flood” as the doctrine and the cross line as the optional one.

## Why this overall label

The song tells the church that the blood of Jesus washes guilt and that he bore the cross the singer should have borne, a ransom for sin. It also asks the church to say “I will always trust,” and it never says he rose. With both stanzas sung, and with the resurrection somewhere else in the hour, this is a song the church can use often.
