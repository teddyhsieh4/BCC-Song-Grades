How we grade

The rubric

We treat the words as the main thing and the music as the servant of the text. We should test the lyrics we sing about God just as much as we attempt to test the Word we read about God.

Guiding principles

The method of evaluation tries to emulate Bob Kauflin’s Worship Matters. A song should help the room remember, proclaim, and celebrate God’s worthiness, works, and Word. Affection grows best when the lyric first tells the truth about God and the gospel. We look for language that is God-centered, corporate, clear, and theologically substantial. Vague spirituality and songs that live mainly in private feeling sit lower.

Red flags (Avoid, or heavy caution)

  • Prosperity gospel or health-and-wealth implications
  • Charismatic excess, or language that treats private revelation as weightier than the written Word
  • Romanticized “Jesus is my boyfriend” intimacy language
  • Minimizing of sin, repentance, or substitutionary atonement
  • A heavily man-centered emphasis (lots of "I will do this")
  • Strong association with or a direct product of Hillsong, Elevation, or Bethel (these ministries are never used)
  • Vague spiritual language a non-Christian could sing without being challenged

Five scores (1–5 each)

Orthodoxy is the starting line. A lyric can be free of heresy and still land at a 3.

  1. Theological Accuracy and Biblical Fidelity (highest weight). Does the song stay true to the whole counsel of Scripture? Precision matters. An impressionistic line such as “darkness tries to hide” is weaker than language you can pin to a text.
  2. Christ-Exalting and Gospel-Saturated. Does the lyric hold both the person and the finished work of Christ? Titles without the cross, substitution, resurrection, or mediation usually land at 2 or 3.
  3. Objectivity vs. Subjectivity. Does the song mainly declare truths about God and Christ, or does it stay inside personal feeling?
  4. Description of God’s Character. Are the attributes balanced, or does love and feeling crowd out holiness, justice, and sovereignty?
  5. Congregational Usefulness and Clarity. Can the body sing it and also be taught by it? An easy hook is not enough for a 4 or 5.

What the numbers mean

  • 5 dense, precise, Scripture-shaped; models what this church wants to sing
  • 4 solid and usable; minor gaps, still teaches
  • 3 true but thin, vague, or incomplete; easier for a seeker room than for a Berean one
  • 2 serious omission or imbalance (missing gospel, or mushy poetry in place of doctrine)
  • 1 error, man-centered core, or disqualifying confusion

Overall recommendation

  • Excellent / Prefer gold-standard texts; feature freely (Getty, CityAlight, Sovereign Grace, In Christ Alone, Before the Throne, Is He Worthy, Yet Not I, and songs like them)
  • Strong rich enough to use regularly; small notes only
  • Acceptable with notes orthodox and usable sparingly when the rest of the set supplies what the song lacks
  • Weak popular, generically true, low rigor; do not feature. At most a brief filler after Scripture has already done the work. How Great Is Our God (Tomlin / Cash / Reeves, CCLI 4348399) is the calibration example of Weak.
  • Avoid red-flag theology or banned-ministry association

If a song works mainly because it is easy, emotional, and broadly theistic, keep it at Weak or low Acceptable.

How a report is written

Every song gets a written analysis, not only a score. This means there will be lyric quotes and reviewing how they connect to Scripture. Including potential setlist notes: sermon placement, pairings, thematic strength.
